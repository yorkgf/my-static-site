Course Overview and Science Practices

SCIENCE PRACTICES

The seven science practices spiral across every unit — they are revisited and built upon throughout the course, from the first motion graphs in Unit 1 to the fluid-flow arguments of Unit 8. The College Board groups these into two science practice categories: Practice 1 (Visual Representations) and Practices 2–4 (Question and Method, Representing Data and Phenomena, Data Analysis) and Practices 5–7 (Theoretical Relationships, Mathematical Routines, Argumentation).

  • 1 Visual Representations
    Create diagrams, tables, charts, or schematics to represent physical situations; create quantitative graphs with appropriate scales and units; create qualitative sketches of graphs that represent the behavior of a physical system.
  • 2 Question and Method
    Derive symbolic expressions from known quantities by selecting and following a logical mathematical pathway; calculate or estimate unknown quantities with units; compare physical quantities between scenarios; predict new values using functional dependence between variables.
  • 3 Representing Data and Phenomena
    Create experimental procedures appropriate for a given scientific question; apply an appropriate law, definition, theoretical relationship, or model to make a claim; justify or support a claim using evidence from experimental data, physical representations, or physical principles.
  • 4 Data Analysis
    Select and use appropriate measurement tools, sources of error, and uncertainty; analyze and interpret data; draw and justify conclusions from experimental data.
  • 5 Theoretical Relationships
    Apply theoretical relationships to analyze physical systems; connect theories/models across representations; evaluate scientific questions using physical principles.
  • 6 Mathematical Routines
    Select and use mathematical relationships to solve problems; use equations, symbols, and units correctly; interpret and apply functional relationships.
  • 7 Argumentation
    Justify or support claims with evidence and reasoning; construct scientific arguments; evaluate alternative explanations and use physics principles to defend a position.

BIG IDEAS

Four big ideas spiral across the entire course. Students revisit them in every unit, connecting the content of the course into a coherent story:

  • CHA Change — Interactions produce changes in motion, energy, and momentum. Kinematics, dynamics, energy, and momentum all describe change.
  • INT Force Interactions — Forces arise from interactions between objects and systems; they change motion, transfer energy, and conserve momentum.
  • FLD Fields — Fields explain interactions at a distance (gravity in AP Physics 1); they mediate force interactions without contact.
  • CON Conservation — Energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum are conserved in isolated systems, providing powerful tools for predicting motion.

AP Physics 1 is an algebra-based, introductory college-level physics course. Students cultivate their understanding of physics through classroom study, in-class activity, and hands-on, inquiry-based laboratory work as they explore concepts like systems, fields, force interactions, change, and conservation.

Unit 1 - Kinematics (10-15%)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe a scalar or vector quantity using magnitude and direction, as appropriate, and distinguish between them.
  • Describe a change in an object's position using displacement, and distinguish it from distance traveled.
  • Describe the average and instantaneous velocity and acceleration of an object using algebraic relationships.
  • Represent constant-velocity and constant-acceleration motion in words, graphs, equations, and diagrams — and translate fluidly between these representations.
  • Use reference frames to describe relative motion and explain why two observers can give conflicting but correct descriptions.
  • Analyze motion in two dimensions using vector components, including projectile motion under constant gravitational acceleration.
  • Create and interpret x-t, v-t, and a-t graphs: slopes, areas, intercepts, and shape of each.
  • Use the kinematic equations to predict position, velocity, and acceleration of objects with constant acceleration.

  • Progress Check 1: Multiple-choice ~18 questions; Free-response 4 questions.

Guiding Questions

  • How can the idea of frames of reference allow two people to tell the truth yet have conflicting reports?
  • How can we estimate the height of a very tall building with only a small rock and a stopwatch?
  • Why might it seem like you are moving backwards when a car passes you on the highway?
  • Why is the general rule for stopping your car "when you double your speed, you must give yourself four times as much distance to stop"?
  • What does the slope of an x-t graph tell us? What does the area under a v-t graph tell us?
  • Why does a dropped ball and a thrown ball hit the ground at the same time (when launched horizontally from the same height)?

Key Concepts

  • Scalars vs. Vectors: Scalars (distance, speed) have magnitude only; vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration) have magnitude and direction.
  • Displacement: Change in position, Δx = x − x₀; independent of path taken.
  • Average vs. Instantaneous: Average velocity = Δx/Δt; instantaneous velocity is the limit as Δt → 0 (slope of tangent).
  • Constant Acceleration: The kinematic equations v = v₀ + at, x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at², v² = v₀² + 2aΔx.
  • Graphical Analysis: Slope of x-t → velocity; slope of v-t → acceleration; area under v-t → displacement; area under a-t → change in velocity.
  • Reference Frames: Relative velocity v_AB = v_A − v_B; observers in different frames describe motion differently but consistently.
  • Projectile Motion: Horizontal and vertical components independent; horizontal velocity constant; vertical acceleration = g.

Key Vocabulary

  • Scalar: A quantity described by magnitude only (e.g., distance, speed, time, mass).
  • Vector: A quantity described by both magnitude and direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, acceleration, force).
  • Position: The location of an object relative to a reference point, specified by a coordinate.
  • Displacement: The change in an object's position; a vector quantity.
  • Velocity: The rate of change of position with time; a vector (speed with direction).
  • Acceleration: The rate of change of velocity with time; a vector. Can be nonzero even when speed is constant (direction change).
  • Free Fall: Motion under the influence of gravity alone, with constant acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s².
  • Projectile: An object moving through the air under the influence of gravity only.
  • Reference Frame: A coordinate system relative to which motion is described.
  • Trajectory: The path of a projectile — a parabola for ideal projectile motion.

Common Mistakes

Misconceptions about Motion

  • Believing a negative acceleration always means slowing down — direction matters, not just sign.
  • Confusing velocity and acceleration ("constant speed" vs. "no acceleration" in circular motion).
  • Thinking an object with zero velocity must have zero acceleration (instantaneous rest at turnaround points).
  • Mixing up distance and displacement in word problems.

Graph Errors

  • Reading slope of an x-t graph as acceleration instead of velocity.
  • Using area under a-t graph for displacement instead of change in velocity.
  • Sketching x-t graphs as straight lines for accelerated motion (should be parabolic).
  • Forgetting that horizontal and vertical motion are independent in projectile problems.

Unit 2 - Force and Translational Dynamics (18-23%)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe a force as an interaction between two objects or systems, and identify the two interacting objects for every force.
  • Describe the properties and interactions of a system, and model the system as a single object at its center of mass.
  • Calculate the location of a system's center of mass for systems of up to five particles in two dimensions.
  • Draw and analyze free-body diagrams showing all forces exerted on an object or system.
  • Apply Newton's First Law: an object at rest or in uniform motion stays that way unless acted on by a net external force.
  • Apply Newton's Second Law: ΣF = ma to solve for acceleration, force, or mass in one and two dimensions.
  • Apply Newton's Third Law: forces always come in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on different objects.
  • Analyze gravitational, normal, tension, static friction, kinetic friction, and spring forces quantitatively.
  • Analyze uniform circular motion: net force directed toward the center, centripetal acceleration v²/r.

  • This unit contributes to a 18% to 23% weight in the AP exam — the largest weighting of any unit.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 2.1–2.9

  • Science Practices: Practices 1, 2, and 3 — translation between models and representations is key.
  • Progress Check 2: Multiple-choice ~30 questions; Free-response 4 questions.
  • Free-response Q4 (Qualitative/Quantitative Translation) frequently draws on this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • Why do we feel pulled toward Earth but not toward a pencil?
  • Why is it more difficult to stop a fully loaded dump truck than a small passenger car?
  • Why is it difficult to walk on ice?
  • Why will a delivery truck filled with birds sitting on its floor have the same weight as a truck with the same birds flying around inside?
  • What is the difference between mass and weight, and why does your weight change on the Moon but your mass does not?
  • If a heavy and a light object are dropped together, why do they fall at the same rate (ignoring air resistance)?
  • Why do you feel pushed against the door when a car turns left? Is there really a force pushing you outward?

Key Concepts

  • Systems: A system is a chosen set of objects; forces are either internal (between system parts) or external (from the environment).
  • Free-Body Diagrams: Visual representation of all external forces acting on an object; the starting point for applying Newton's Second Law.
  • Newton's First Law: An object's velocity is constant unless a net external force acts — defines the concept of inertia.
  • Newton's Second Law: ΣF = ma; the net force determines acceleration, not velocity.
  • Newton's Third Law: Force pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on different objects.
  • Friction: Static friction adjusts up to a maximum (μ_sN); kinetic friction is constant (μ_kN) opposing relative motion.
  • Spring Forces: Hooke's law, F = −kx, where x is displacement from equilibrium.
  • Circular Motion: Net force toward center provides centripetal acceleration a_c = v²/r = ω²r.

Key Vocabulary

  • System: A chosen collection of objects being studied; everything else is the environment.
  • Force: An interaction between two objects that can cause a change in motion.
  • Net Force: The vector sum of all external forces acting on an object or system.
  • Inertia: The tendency of an object to resist changes in its velocity; quantified by mass.
  • Normal Force: The perpendicular contact force exerted by a surface on an object.
  • Friction: A force that opposes relative motion (or tendency of motion) between surfaces in contact.
  • Tension: The pulling force transmitted through a rope, cable, or string.
  • Gravitational Force: F_g = mg near Earth's surface; the field (FLD) interaction that gives weight.
  • Centripetal Force: The net inward force required to maintain uniform circular motion — not a new force type.
  • Center of Mass: The point at which a system's mass may be considered concentrated; x_cm = Σmᵢxᵢ/Σmᵢ.

Common Mistakes

Newton's Laws Misconceptions

  • Thinking forces are needed to keep an object moving at constant velocity.
  • Applying Newton's Third Law to forces on the same object (e.g., "action-reaction cancels").
  • Confusing mass and weight; saying weight = m·g is a "force" but forgetting weight is the gravitational force.
  • Including non-existent forces like "centrifugal force" or "force of motion" in FBDs.

Problem-Solving Errors

  • Drawing FBDs with the wrong number of forces or wrong force directions.
  • Forgetting that static friction can be less than its maximum value.
  • Using μ_sN when the object is already moving (should use μ_kN).
  • Mixing up centripetal acceleration direction (always toward center, not outward).
  • Not resolving forces along tilted axes on inclined planes.

Unit 3 - Work, Energy, and Power (18-23%)

Learning Objectives

  • Define work as the dot product of force and displacement: W = F·d = Fd cos θ, and calculate work done by constant and variable forces.
  • Apply the work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔK = ½mv² − ½mv₀².
  • Define and calculate kinetic energy (K = ½mv²) and gravitational potential energy (U_g = mgh).
  • Define and calculate elastic potential energy for a spring: U_s = ½kx².
  • Apply the principle of conservation of mechanical energy: K₁ + U₁ = K₂ + U₂ when only conservative forces act.
  • Analyze situations with non-conservative forces (friction) and account for energy transfer as heat: W_nc = ΔE_mech.
  • Define and calculate power: P = W/t = F·v, and distinguish average from instantaneous power.
  • Use energy bar charts (LOL diagrams) to visualize energy transformations and transfers.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 18 class periods to complete (Weeks 9–12).
  • This unit contributes to a 18% to 23% weight in the AP exam — tied for the largest weighting with Unit 2.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 3.1–3.6
  • Big Ideas: CON (Conservation) and CHA (Change).
  • Science Practices: Practices 2 (Mathematical Routines) and 3 (Argumentation) are emphasized.
  • Progress Check 3: Multiple-choice ~25 questions; Free-response 4 questions.
  • Energy conservation is a recurring theme in Units 3, 5, 6, and 7.

Guiding Questions

  • What is the relationship between work, energy, and power in physical systems?
  • How does the work-energy theorem simplify problem-solving in mechanics?
  • What distinguishes conservative forces from non-conservative forces?
  • How is energy conserved in isolated systems, and when is it not?
  • What are the practical applications of power calculations in engineering?
  • Why does a roller coaster speed up at the bottom of a hill and slow down at the top?
  • Why does a person feel more tired climbing stairs than walking on flat ground?

Key Concepts

  • Work: W = F·d cos θ; work is done only by the component of force parallel to displacement. Positive work adds energy; negative work removes energy.
  • Kinetic Energy: K = ½mv²; the energy of motion. Work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔK.
  • Potential Energy: Stored energy due to position in a force field. U_g = mgh (gravity); U_s = ½kx² (spring).
  • Conservative Forces: Forces for which work is path-independent; mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces act.
  • Non-Conservative Forces: Forces (like friction) for which work depends on path; mechanical energy is not conserved.
  • Conservation of Energy: Total energy in an isolated system is constant: E_total = K + U + E_thermal = constant.
  • Power: P = W/t = F·v; the rate of energy transfer or work done. Units: watts (W) = J/s.
  • LOL Diagrams: Energy bar charts showing initial energy, energy transfers (work), and final energy.

Key Vocabulary

  • Work: The transfer of energy by a force acting through a displacement; W = Fd cos θ.
  • Kinetic Energy (K): The energy an object possesses due to its motion; K = ½mv².
  • Potential Energy (U): Stored energy associated with the position or configuration of an object in a force field.
  • Gravitational Potential Energy: U_g = mgh near Earth's surface; energy stored in the gravitational field.
  • Elastic Potential Energy: U_s = ½kx²; energy stored in a stretched or compressed spring.
  • Mechanical Energy: The sum of kinetic and potential energy: E_mech = K + U.
  • Conservative Force: A force for which the work done is independent of the path taken (gravity, spring force).
  • Non-Conservative Force: A force for which work depends on path (friction, air resistance).
  • Power: The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred; P = W/t.
  • Efficiency: The ratio of useful energy output to total energy input, often expressed as a percentage.

Common Mistakes

Work and Energy Errors

  • Confusing Work and Energy: work is the transfer of energy, not energy itself.
  • Sign errors in work calculations: forgetting that W = Fd cos θ can be negative (force opposite to displacement).
  • Misapplying conservation of energy: including non-conservative forces like friction without accounting for energy loss as heat.
  • Power vs. Energy confusion: mixing up power (rate of energy transfer) with energy (total amount of work done).

Problem-Solving Errors

  • Path Independence Misconception: not understanding that work done by conservative forces is path-independent, while non-conservative forces depend on path.
  • Forgetting to include all forms of energy (K, U_g, U_s) in conservation equations.
  • Using the wrong reference level for gravitational potential energy (h = 0 point).
  • Not checking that answers are physically reasonable (e.g., speed > c).

Unit 4 - Linear Momentum (10-15%)

Learning Objectives

  • Define linear momentum as p = mv and impulse as J = F_net·Δt = Δp.
  • Apply the impulse-momentum theorem: J = Δp = mv_f − mv_i.
  • State the conditions for conservation of linear momentum: the net external force on the system must be zero.
  • Apply conservation of linear momentum to solve one- and two-dimensional collision problems.
  • Distinguish between elastic collisions (both momentum and kinetic energy conserved) and inelastic collisions (only momentum conserved).
  • Analyze perfectly inelastic collisions where objects stick together after collision.
  • Calculate the center of mass of a system and describe its motion under external forces.
  • Use momentum bar charts to visualize momentum transfer in collisions.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 12 class periods to complete (Weeks 13–14).
  • This unit contributes to a 10% to 15% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 4.1–4.4
  • Big Ideas: CON (Conservation) and INT (Force Interactions).
  • Science Practices: Practices 2 (Mathematical Routines) and 3 (Argumentation) are emphasized.
  • Progress Check 4: Multiple-choice ~18 questions; Free-response 4 questions.

Guiding Questions

  • How does the center of mass of a system behave under external forces?
  • What conditions must be met for momentum to be conserved in a system?
  • How do elastic and inelastic collisions differ in terms of energy conservation?
  • What techniques are used to analyze two-dimensional collision problems?
  • How does the concept of impulse relate to changes in momentum?
  • Why does a gun recoil when fired? How does this demonstrate momentum conservation?
  • Why is it harder to catch a fast-moving baseball than a slow one, even if they have the same mass?

Key Concepts

  • Linear Momentum: p = mv; a vector quantity conserved in isolated systems (no net external force).
  • Impulse: J = F_net·Δt = Δp; the change in momentum resulting from a force applied over time.
  • Impulse-Momentum Theorem: The impulse exerted on an object equals its change in momentum.
  • Conservation of Momentum: Σp_before = Σp_after when the net external force is zero.
  • Elastic Collisions: Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. Objects bounce off each other.
  • Inelastic Collisions: Momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not (converted to heat, sound, deformation).
  • Perfectly Inelastic Collisions: Objects stick together after collision; maximum kinetic energy loss.
  • Center of Mass: The point where the total mass of a system can be considered concentrated; x_cm = Σmᵢxᵢ/Σmᵢ.

Key Vocabulary

  • Linear Momentum (p): The product of an object's mass and velocity; p = mv. A vector quantity.
  • Impulse (J): The product of the average net force and the time interval over which it acts; J = F_net·Δt = Δp.
  • Conservation of Momentum: The total momentum of an isolated system remains constant when no net external force acts.
  • Elastic Collision: A collision in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.
  • Inelastic Collision: A collision in which momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not.
  • Perfectly Inelastic Collision: A collision in which the colliding objects stick together after impact.
  • Center of Mass: The average position of all mass in a system, weighted by mass.
  • System: The collection of objects being studied; internal forces cancel in momentum conservation.

Common Mistakes

Momentum Misconceptions

  • Confusing Momentum and Energy Conservation: momentum is always conserved in isolated systems, but kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions.
  • Vector Direction Mistakes: forgetting that momentum is a vector quantity and not properly accounting for directions in collision problems, especially in 2D.
  • Impulse Direction Errors: misunderstanding that impulse equals the change in momentum, leading to sign errors.
  • Elastic vs. Inelastic Confusion: not recognizing that inelastic collisions involve energy loss while elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy.

Problem-Solving Errors

  • Center of Mass Calculation Errors: incorrectly calculating center of mass by not properly weighting each mass by its position.
  • Sign errors in 1D collision equations (momentum is a vector).
  • Forgetting to include all objects in the system when applying conservation of momentum.
  • Using conservation of kinetic energy in inelastic collision problems.

Unit 5 - Torque and Rotational Dynamics (10-15%)

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the relationships between angular position, angular velocity, and angular acceleration and their linear counterparts.
  • Calculate angular displacement, velocity, and acceleration for constant angular acceleration using rotational kinematic equations.
  • Define torque as τ = rF sin θ or τ = r_perp·F, and calculate torque about a chosen pivot point.
  • Apply the rotational analog of Newton's Second Law: τ_net = Iα, where I is the moment of inertia.
  • Calculate the moment of inertia I for point masses (I = Σmr²) and for common geometric shapes using standard formulas.
  • Analyze systems in rotational equilibrium: τ_net = 0 (no angular acceleration).
  • Apply the parallel-axis theorem: I = I_cm + Md² for rotation about a non-center-of-mass axis.
  • Solve problems involving combined translational and rotational motion (rolling without slipping: v = ωR).

  • What role does torque play in rotational motion, and how is it calculated?
  • How does the moment of inertia affect rotational motion and energy?

Key Concepts

  • Rotational Kinematics: Angular displacement θ, angular velocity ω = dθ/dt, angular acceleration α = dω/dt. Equations mirror linear kinematics with angular quantities.
  • Torque (τ): The rotational equivalent of force; τ = rF sin θ = r_perp·F. Direction by right-hand rule.
  • Moment of Inertia (I): The rotational equivalent of mass; I = Σmr² for point masses. Depends on mass distribution relative to the rotation axis.
  • Rotational Dynamics: τ_net = Iα (Newton's Second Law for rotation).
  • Rotational Equilibrium: τ_net = 0; no angular acceleration. Used for static systems (seesaws, balanced beams).
  • Parallel-Axis Theorem: I = I_cm + Md² for rotation about an axis parallel to one through the CM.
  • Rolling Without Slipping: v = ωR; static friction provides the torque for angular acceleration.

Key Vocabulary

  • Angular Displacement (θ): The angle through which an object rotates; measured in radians.
  • Angular Velocity (ω): The rate of change of angular displacement; ω = dθ/dt. Units: rad/s.
  • Angular Acceleration (α): The rate of change of angular velocity; α = dω/dt. Units: rad/s².
  • Torque (τ): A force that causes rotation; τ = rF sin θ. Units: N·m.
  • Moment of Inertia (I): A measure of an object's resistance to changes in its rotational motion; depends on mass distribution.
  • Rotational Equilibrium: A state in which the net torque on an object is zero and it has no angular acceleration.
  • Lever Arm (r_perp): The perpendicular distance from the pivot point to the line of action of the force.
  • Rolling Without Slipping: A condition where the point of contact with the surface is instantaneously at rest; v = ωR.

Unit 6 - Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems (5-8%)

Learning Objectives

  • Define and calculate rotational kinetic energy: K_rot = ½Iω², and include it in conservation of energy problems.
  • Calculate the work done by a torque: W = τθ (for constant torque) and relate it to the work-energy theorem for rotation.
  • Define angular momentum as L = Iω for a rigid body and L = r × p = rpsin θ for a point mass.
  • State the conditions for conservation of angular momentum: the net external torque on the system must be zero.
  • Apply conservation of angular momentum: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ when τ_net = 0.
  • Analyze scenarios where moment of inertia changes (ice skater pulling arms in; collapsing star).
  • Solve combined translation + rotation problems using both linear and angular momentum conservation.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 10 class periods to complete (Weeks 18–19, post-Winter Break).
  • This unit contributes to a 5% to 8% weight in the AP exam — the smallest weighting.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 6.1–6.3
  • Big Ideas: CON (Conservation) and CHA (Change).
  • Science Practices: Practices 2 (Mathematical Routices) and 3 (Argumentation) are emphasized.
  • Progress Check 6: Multiple-choice ~10 questions; Free-response 2 questions.

Guiding Questions

  • How is angular momentum related to linear momentum and rotational motion?
  • Under what conditions is angular momentum conserved in physical systems?
  • How does the rate of change of angular momentum relate to applied torques (τ = dL/dt)?
  • What are the implications of angular momentum conservation in astronomy (collapsing stars, planetary orbits)?
  • How do gyroscopes and spinning objects demonstrate angular momentum principles?
  • Why does a figure skater spin faster when pulling her arms in?

Key Concepts

  • Rotational Kinetic Energy: K_rot = ½Iω²; included in total mechanical energy for rolling objects: E_mech = K_trans + K_rot + U.
  • Work by Torque: W = τθ (for constant torque); the rotational work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔK_rot.
  • Angular Momentum (L): L = Iω for rigid bodies; L = rpsin θ for point masses. A vector quantity.
  • Conservation of Angular Momentum: L_initial = L_final when τ_net = 0. I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.
  • Torque-Angular Momentum Relation: τ_net = dL/dt (Newton's Second Law for rotation in angular momentum form).
  • Gyroscopic Effects: Spinning objects resist changes to their orientation due to angular momentum conservation.

Key Vocabulary

  • Rotational Kinetic Energy (K_rot): The energy an object possesses due to its rotation; K_rot = ½Iω².
  • Angular Momentum (L): The rotational equivalent of linear momentum; L = Iω for rigid bodies.
  • Conservation of Angular Momentum: The total angular momentum of an isolated system remains constant when no net external torque acts.
  • Gyroscope: A spinning wheel mounted so its axis can rotate freely; demonstrates angular momentum conservation.
  • Precession: The slow change in the orientation of the rotation axis of a spinning object due to an external torque.
  • Right-Hand Rule: A convention for determining the direction of angular velocity, angular momentum, and torque vectors.

Unit 7 - Oscillations (5-8%)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe simple harmonic motion (SHM) as oscillatory motion where the restoring force is proportional to displacement: F = −kx.
  • Define and relate period (T), frequency (f), and angular frequency (ω): ω = 2πf = 2π/T.
  • Derive and apply the equations for position, velocity, and acceleration in SHM: x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ).
  • Analyze the energy of a simple harmonic oscillator: E_total = ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx² (constant).
  • Analyze and calculate the period and frequency of a simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) (for small angles).
  • Analyze and calculate the period and frequency of a mass-spring system: T = 2π√(m/k).
  • Describe how amplitude, mass, spring constant, pendulum length, and gravity affect period and frequency.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 8 class periods to complete (Weeks 20–21).
  • This unit contributes to a 5% to 8% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 7.1–7.4
  • Big Ideas: CHA (Change) and CON (Conservation).
  • Science Practices: Practices 1 (Visual Representations), 2 (Mathematical Routines), and 3 (Argumentation) are emphasized.
  • Progress Check 7: Multiple-choice ~10 questions; Free-response 2 questions.

Guiding Questions

  • What conditions produce simple harmonic motion?
  • How do we describe the position, velocity, and acceleration of an oscillator as functions of time?
  • How is energy conserved in an oscillating system, and how does it shift between kinetic and potential forms?
  • What determines the period and frequency of a mass-spring system? Of a pendulum?
  • Why does a pendulum's period depend on length but not on mass?
  • Why does a guitar string produce a specific pitch? How does this relate to SHM?

Key Concepts

  • Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): Oscillatory motion with restoring force F = −kx (proportional to displacement, opposite direction). Solutions: x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ).
  • Period (T) and Frequency (f): T = 1/f; ω = 2πf = 2π/T. Period is time for one complete cycle.
  • Mass-Spring System: T = 2π√(m/k); frequency increases with k, decreases with m.
  • Simple Pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g) (small-angle approximation); period is independent of mass and amplitude.
  • Energy in SHM: E_total = ½kA² = constant; energy oscillates between K = ½mv² and U = ½kx².
  • Amplitude (A): The maximum displacement from equilibrium; determines total energy (E ∝ A²).

Key Vocabulary

  • Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): Periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement and opposite in direction.
  • Oscillation: Repeated back-and-forth motion about an equilibrium position.
  • Period (T): The time required to complete one full cycle of motion. Units: seconds.
  • Frequency (f): The number of cycles per unit time; f = 1/T. Units: Hz (1/s).
  • Angular Frequency (ω): ω = 2πf = 2π/T. Units: rad/s.
  • Amplitude (A): The maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
  • Restoring Force: A force that acts to return an object to its equilibrium position; F = −kx for SHM.
  • Pendulum: A mass suspended from a pivot that swings freely under gravity; approximates SHM for small angles.

Common Mistakes

SHM Misconceptions

  • Thinking a pendulum's period depends on mass (it does not for a simple pendulum).
  • Confusing period (T) with frequency (f): T = 1/f, not T = f.
  • Using the small-angle approximation for large-amplitude pendulum problems.

Problem-Solving Errors

  • Using the wrong equation for period: T = 2π√(m/k) for springs, T = 2π√(L/g) for pendulums.

Unit 8 - Fluids (10-15%)

Learning Objectives

  • Define density (ρ = m/V) and pressure (P = F/A), and calculate pressure at a depth in a fluid: P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Apply Pascal's principle: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to all parts of the fluid.
  • Apply Archimedes' principle: the buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid displaced: F_b = ρ_fluid·V_displaced·g.
  • Determine whether an object floats or sinks based on density comparison and force balance.
  • Define and apply the equation of continuity for incompressible fluid flow: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (conservation of mass).
  • Apply Bernoulli's equation: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline (conservation of energy for fluids).
  • Analyze real fluid behavior including viscosity and turbulence (qualitative understanding).

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 10 class periods to complete (Weeks 21–22).
  • This unit contributes to a 10% to 15% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 8.1–8.4
  • Big Ideas: INT (Force Interactions) and CON (Conservation).
  • Science Practices: Practices 2 (Mathematical Routines) and 3 (Argumentation) are emphasized.
  • Progress Check 8: Multiple-choice ~15 questions; Free-response 3 questions.
  • Note: Fluids is the only unit that introduces the FLD (Fields) big idea in depth (pressure as a field).

Guiding Questions

  • Why do some objects float while others sink in water?
  • How does water pressure change as you dive deeper into a pool?
  • How does a hydraulic lift work to amplify force?
  • Why does a fast-moving stream of air create low pressure (Bernoulli's principle)?
  • How do airplanes generate lift? How does this relate to Bernoulli's equation?
  • Why does a curved baseball curve in flight? How does spin affect fluid flow?

Key Concepts

  • Pressure (P): Force per unit area; P = F/A. Units: Pa = N/m². In a fluid at rest, pressure increases with depth: P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Pascal's Principle: Pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid. Basis of hydraulic systems.
  • Buoyancy: Upward force on an object in a fluid, equal to the weight of the displaced fluid (Archimedes' principle): F_b = ρ_fluid·V_displaced·g.
  • Equation of Continuity: For incompressible flow, A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (mass conservation). Flow rate Q = Av = constant.
  • Bernoulli's Equation: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline. Explains lift, venturi effect, and atomizers.
  • Viscosity: A fluid's resistance to flow; causes energy loss in real fluid systems.

Key Vocabulary

  • Density (ρ): Mass per unit volume; ρ = m/V. Units: kg/m³.
  • Pressure (P): Force per unit area; P = F/A. Units: pascals (Pa) = N/m².
  • Buoyant Force (F_b): The upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object; F_b = ρ_fluid·V_displaced·g.
  • Specific Gravity: The ratio of a substance's density to the density of water; dimensionless.
  • Flow Rate (Q): The volume of fluid flowing per unit time; Q = Av. Units: m³/s.
  • Bernoulli's Principle: In a flowing fluid, an increase in velocity is accompanied by a decrease in pressure or potential energy.
  • Viscosity: A measure of a fluid's resistance to flow; "thickness" of a fluid.
  • Turbulence: Chaotic, irregular fluid flow (vs. laminar flow, which is smooth and orderly).

Common Mistakes

Fluids Misconceptions

  • Confusing Pressure and Force: pressure is force per unit area (P = F/A), not force itself.
  • Thinking buoyant force depends on the object's mass or shape (it depends only on displaced fluid volume).
  • Believing pressure acts only downward (fluid pressure acts in all directions, increasing with depth).
  • Confusing Bernoulli's principle: high velocity → low pressure (not high pressure).

Problem-Solving Errors

  • Forgetting atmospheric pressure (P₀) when calculating absolute pressure at depth.
  • Not converting units properly (1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³).
  • Applying Bernoulli's equation to turbulent flow or when viscosity is significant.
