Course Overview and Statistical Practices

STATISTICAL PRACTICES

The four statistical practices spiral across every unit — they are revisited and built upon throughout the course, from the first investigation in Unit 1 to the inference procedures of Units 3–5.

  • 1 Formulate Questions
    Determine valid investigative questions that require statistical investigation; identify information needed to answer questions.
  • 2 Collect Data
    Justify appropriate methods for ethically gathering and representing data through random sampling and experimental design.
  • 3 Analyze Data
    Construct tabular and graphical representations; calculate summary statistics, probabilities, and inference results.
  • 4 Interpret Results
    Describe distributions, justify claims based on statistical calculations, verify conditions for inference methods, and interpret results in context.

STATISTICAL SKILLS (SPIRALED ACROSS UNITS)

The College Board skill categories that students practice and master throughout the course:

  • 1.A Valid investigative questions requiring statistics
  • 2.A Identify information needed to answer questions
  • 2.B Justify data collection methods
  • 3.A–3.E Tables & graphs; summary statistics; probabilities; parameters; inference calculations
  • 4.A–4.G Describe distributions; justify claims; verify conditions; interpret inference in context

All inference procedures follow the Four-Step Inference Process (State, Plan, Do, Conclude) — introduced in Unit 3 and used on every FRQ thereafter.

Unit 1 - Exploring One-Variable Data and Collecting Data (20-30%)

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the components of a statistical study and determine what can be learned from data.
  • Distinguish between categorical and quantitative variables and choose appropriate representations for each.
  • Construct and interpret frequency tables, bar charts, pie charts, dotplots, stemplots, histograms, and boxplots.
  • Describe distributions of quantitative data using shape, center, variability, and unusual features (SOCS).
  • Calculate and interpret summary statistics: mean, median, range, IQR, and standard deviation.
  • Compare distributions of quantitative variables side-by-side using z-scores and percentiles.
  • Distinguish between observational studies and experiments, and identify potential problems with sampling.
  • Apply the principles of experimental design: control, randomization, and replication.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 24 class periods to complete (Weeks 1–7).
  • This unit contributes to a 20% to 30% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 1.1–1.13
  • Statistical Practices: All four practices (Formulate Questions, Collect Data, Analyze Data, Interpret Results) are engaged in this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • What kind of questions can be answered using data?
  • What methods should I use to collect data for future inference procedures?
  • How do we choose the appropriate graph or table for a given variable type?
  • How do we describe and compare distributions of quantitative data?
  • What is the difference between an observational study and an experiment?
  • Why is randomization important in sampling and experimental design?
  • What are the potential problems with sampling, and how can we avoid bias?

Key Concepts

  • Variable Types: Categorical vs. quantitative variables and the representations appropriate for each.
  • Graphical Representations: Frequency tables, bar charts, pie charts (categorical); dotplots, stemplots, histograms, boxplots (quantitative).
  • SOCS: Shape, Outliers, Center, Spread — the standard framework for describing distributions.
  • Summary Statistics: Mean, median, range, IQR, standard deviation; resistant measures (median, IQR) vs. non-resistant (mean, SD).
  • z-Scores: Standardized scores measuring position relative to the mean in standard deviation units.
  • Random Sampling: SRS, stratified, cluster, and systematic sampling; census vs. sample.
  • Experimental Design: Control, randomization, replication; confounding variables.
  • Sampling Bias: Voluntary response, undercoverage, nonresponse, and response bias.

Key Vocabulary

  • Categorical Variable: A variable that takes on category labels (e.g., color, gender, region).
  • Quantitative Variable: A variable that takes on numerical values that can be averaged or ordered (e.g., height, score).
  • Distribution: The pattern of variation of a variable over all individuals or observations.
  • Center: A typical or representative value of a distribution (mean or median).
  • Spread/Variability: The extent to which values in a distribution vary (range, IQR, standard deviation).
  • Outlier: A value that falls markedly outside the overall pattern of the data.
  • Observational Study: A study that observes individuals and measures variables without imposing treatment.
  • Experiment: A study that deliberately imposes a treatment on individuals to measure response.
  • Simple Random Sample (SRS): A sample where every set of n individuals has an equal chance of selection.
  • Confounding: When two variables' effects on a response cannot be distinguished.

Unit 2 - Probability, Random Variables, and Probability Distributions (15-25%)

Learning Objectives

  • Construct and interpret two-way tables, including marginal and conditional distributions for two categorical variables.
  • Estimate probabilities using simulation with random number generators.
  • Apply the basic probability rules: complement, addition, multiplication, and general addition rules.
  • Determine whether events are mutually exclusive, independent, or conditional.
  • Define and compute the mean (expected value) and standard deviation of discrete random variables.
  • Recognize the binomial setting and calculate binomial probabilities.
  • Use the Normal distribution: standard normal, z-scores, normalcdf, and invNorm.
  • Describe the sampling distribution of the sample mean and apply the Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 24 class periods to complete (Weeks 7–12).
  • This unit contributes to a 15% to 25% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 2.1–2.12
  • Statistical Practices: Analyze Data (probabilities and random variables) and Interpret Results are emphasized.

Guiding Questions

  • How can we use probability to model uncertainty?
  • What is the distribution of sample statistics from repeated sampling?
  • How do we determine whether two events are independent?
  • How do random variables summarize chance outcomes numerically?
  • When is a situation modeled by the binomial distribution, and how do we compute binomial probabilities?
  • How do we use the Normal distribution to answer questions about data?
  • Why does the Central Limit Theorem justify using Normal distributions for sample means?

Key Concepts

  • Two-Way Tables: Marginal and conditional distributions for two categorical variables.
  • Simulation: Using random number generators to estimate probabilities empirically.
  • Probability Rules: Complement rule, addition rule, general addition rule, multiplication rule.
  • Conditional Probability: P(A|B) and the independence misconception (independence ≠ mutually exclusive).
  • Random Variables: Discrete vs. continuous; probability distributions; expected value; standard deviation.
  • Binomial Distribution: Binomial setting, conditions, and probability calculations.
  • Normal Distribution: Standard normal, z-scores, normalcdf, invNorm.
  • Sampling Distributions: Sampling distribution of x̄; Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

Key Vocabulary

  • Probability: A number between 0 and 1 that describes the likelihood of an event occurring.
  • Sample Space: The set of all possible outcomes of a chance process.
  • Mutually Exclusive: Two events that cannot occur at the same time.
  • Independent Events: Events where knowing one occurs does not change the probability of the other.
  • Conditional Probability: The probability of one event given that another event has occurred.
  • Random Variable: A variable whose value is a numerical outcome of a random phenomenon.
  • Expected Value: The mean of a random variable, representing the long-run average outcome.
  • Binomial Setting: Fixed number of independent trials, two outcomes, constant probability of success.
  • Sampling Distribution: The distribution of values taken by a statistic in all possible samples of the same size.
  • Central Limit Theorem: For large samples, the sampling distribution of x̄ is approximately Normal, regardless of the population shape.

  • Analyze Data (Probability): 3.C 3.D

Common Mistakes

Probability Misconceptions

  • Treating mutually exclusive events as independent (or vice versa).
  • Using the multiplication rule when events are not independent.
  • Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) or with P(A ∩ B).

Distribution Errors

  • Applying the binomial model when trials are not independent or n is not fixed.
  • Forgetting to standardize with z-scores before using the Normal table.
  • Using CLT when the sample size is too small and the population is not Normal.

Unit 3 - Inference for Categorical Data: Proportions (15-25%)

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between point estimators and interval estimators for population proportions.
  • Describe the sampling distribution of the sample proportion p̂ and its conditions.
  • Construct and interpret confidence intervals for a population proportion, including margin of error.
  • Set up and carry out hypothesis tests for a population proportion using the four-step inference process.
  • Define and interpret p-values, and explain the meaning of statistical significance.
  • Describe Type I and Type II errors and the concept of power.
  • Construct and interpret confidence intervals and tests for the difference between two population proportions.
  • Set up and carry out chi-square tests for homogeneity and independence.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 28 class periods to complete (Weeks 13–17).
  • This unit contributes to a 15% to 25% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 3.1–3.15
  • Statistical Practices: Analyze Data (inference calculations) and Interpret Results (justify conclusions) are the focus.

Guiding Questions

  • How can we use sample data to make inferences about population proportions?
  • How do we determine whether observed differences are statistically significant?
  • What does a confidence interval tell us about the population, and what does it not tell us?
  • How do we choose between a one-proportion and two-proportion procedure?
  • When do we use a chi-square test for homogeneity versus a chi-square test for independence?
  • What is the relationship between p-value, α, and the conclusion of a hypothesis test?

Key Concepts

  • Sampling Distribution of p̂: Shape, center, and variability; conditions for Normal approximation.
  • Confidence Intervals: Point estimate ± margin of error; interpretation in context; confidence level.
  • Hypothesis Testing: H₀ vs. Hₐ; test statistic; p-value; significance level α; decision and conclusion.
  • Type I & Type II Errors: False positive, false negative; power of a test.
  • Two-Proportion Inference: Sampling distribution of p̂₁ − p̂₂; z-interval; z-test.
  • Chi-Square Tests: Homogeneity, independence, goodness-of-fit; expected counts; chi-square statistic and degrees of freedom.
  • Conditions for Inference: Random, Normal/Large Counts, Independent (10% condition).

Key Vocabulary

  • Point Estimator: A statistic that provides a single best guess for a population parameter (e.g., p̂ for p).
  • Margin of Error: Half the width of a confidence interval; the maximum expected difference between the statistic and parameter.
  • Confidence Interval: An interval of plausible values for a parameter, with a stated level of confidence.
  • Hypothesis: A statement about a population parameter. H₀ (null) is the claim being tested; Hₐ (alternative) is the claim we seek evidence for.
  • p-Value: The probability of getting a sample result at least as extreme as the observed one, assuming H₀ is true.
  • Statistical Significance: When the p-value is less than α, the result is statistically significant.
  • Type I Error: Rejecting H₀ when H₀ is actually true (false positive).
  • Type II Error: Failing to reject H₀ when H₀ is actually false (false negative).
  • Power: The probability that a test correctly rejects H₀ when Hₐ is true.
  • Chi-Square Statistic: Σ(observed − expected)²/expected; used for categorical data inference.

The Four-Step Inference Process (State, Plan, Do, Conclude)

All inference procedures — confidence intervals and hypothesis tests — follow this consistent four-step structure. It is introduced here in Unit 3 and used on every inference FRQ (Q3) on the AP exam.

1. STATE

Parameter of interest in context; hypotheses in symbols and context; confidence level or significance level α.

2. PLAN

Name the procedure; check conditions: Random, Normal/Large Counts, Independent (10% condition).

3. DO

Calculate the test statistic and p-value (or the interval) using the calculator or formula; show the formula and key numbers.

4. CONCLUDE

Compare p-value to α; make a decision (reject or fail to reject H₀); state the conclusion in context — never "accept H₀".

Common Mistakes

Confidence Interval Errors

  • Misinterpreting the confidence level (it is about the method, not a single interval).
  • Forgetting to check the Random and Normal/Large Counts conditions.
  • Concluding causation from an observational study's CI.

Hypothesis Test Errors

  • Writing Hₐ in words instead of symbols, or using x̄ instead of p.
  • Concluding "accept H₀" when failing to reject — this is not evidence H₀ is true.
  • Confusing p-value with the probability that H₀ is true.

Unit 4 - Inference for Quantitative Data: Means (10-20%)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the sampling distribution of the sample mean and the role of the t-distribution and degrees of freedom.
  • Construct and interpret one-sample t-intervals for a population mean.
  • Set up and carry out one-sample t-tests for a population mean using the four-step process.
  • Construct and interpret paired t-intervals and paired t-tests for a mean difference.
  • Describe the sampling distribution for the difference between two sample means.
  • Construct and interpret two-sample t-intervals and two-sample t-tests (using technology for degrees of freedom).
  • Justify claims based on confidence intervals and tests for means and mean differences.
  • Compare and contrast z-procedures and t-procedures and choose the appropriate inference method.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 17 class periods to complete (Weeks 18–21).
  • This unit contributes to a 10% to 20% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 4.1–4.10
  • Statistical Practices: Analyze Data and Interpret Results continue to be emphasized through inference on means.

Guiding Questions

  • How can we evaluate the effectiveness of a new medicine?
  • How can we compare average salaries across different regions?
  • When do we use a t-distribution instead of a Normal distribution?
  • What is the difference between a paired design and an independent two-sample design?
  • How do we check the conditions for t-procedures when the population is not Normal?
  • When is it appropriate to use a z-procedure versus a t-procedure for a mean?

Key Concepts

  • t-Distribution: Symmetric, slightly heavier-tailed than Normal; shape determined by degrees of freedom.
  • One-Sample t-Procedures: t-interval and t-test for a population mean μ.
  • Paired t-Procedures: Inference on a mean difference μ_d when observations are paired (before/after, matched pairs).
  • Two-Sample t-Procedures: Inference on μ₁ − μ₂; degrees of freedom from technology (Welch's approximation).
  • Conditions for t-Procedures: Random sampling/assignment; approximately Normal population or large sample (CLT); independence.
  • z vs. t: z-procedures require known σ; t-procedures use sample standard deviation s and are used when σ is unknown.

Key Vocabulary

  • t-Distribution: A family of distributions used when σ is unknown and the sample standard deviation s estimates it.
  • Degrees of Freedom (df): The number of independent pieces of information (n − 1 for one-sample; approximated by technology for two-sample).
  • Standard Error: The standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution (s/√n for a mean).
  • Paired Data: Data where each observation in one group is matched with a specific observation in the other group.
  • Pooled vs. Unpooled: Two-sample t-procedures on the AP exam use the unpooled (Welch) approach via technology.
  • Robustness: t-procedures are robust to moderate departures from Normality, especially for larger samples.

Common Mistakes

  • Using z-procedures when σ is unknown (should use t).
  • Applying a paired t-test when samples are independent (and vice versa).
  • Using the incorrect degrees of freedom in two-sample procedures.
  • Concluding significance when the CI includes 0 (for a difference) — should be non-significant.
  • Forgetting to state the conclusion in context.

Unit 5 - Regression Analysis (10-20%)

Learning Objectives

  • Construct and interpret scatterplots to display the relationship between two quantitative variables.
  • Describe the direction, form, and strength of a relationship; identify outliers and influential points.
  • Calculate and interpret the correlation coefficient r and understand its properties.
  • Find and interpret the least-squares regression line (LSRL): slope and intercept in context.
  • Calculate and interpret residuals and use residual plots to assess the fit of a linear model.
  • Interpret the coefficient of determination r² as the proportion of variability explained.
  • Distinguish between correlation and causation, and understand the role of lurking variables.
  • Use the regression model to make predictions and evaluate their reasonableness.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 9 class periods to complete (Weeks 21–22).
  • This unit contributes to a 10% to 20% weight in the AP exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 5.1–5.5
  • Statistical Practices: Analyze Data (bivariate representations and calculations) and Interpret Results (justify model fit).

Guiding Questions

  • How can we model the relationship between two quantitative variables?
  • How well does our model fit the data?
  • What does the correlation coefficient tell us about a relationship — and what does it not tell us?
  • How do we interpret the slope and intercept of a regression line in context?
  • Why are residual plots essential for assessing model fit?
  • Can we ever conclude causation from a regression model? Why or why not?

Key Concepts

  • Scatterplots: Displaying bivariate quantitative data; direction, form, strength; outliers and influential points.
  • Correlation (r): Measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship; −1 ≤ r ≤ 1; unitless; not resistant to outliers.
  • Least-Squares Regression Line (LSRL): The line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals; ŷ = a + bx.
  • Residuals: observed − predicted; residual plots for checking linearity.
  • Coefficient of Determination (r²): Proportion of variability in y explained by the linear regression on x.
  • Causation vs. Correlation: Correlation does not imply causation; lurking variables and confounding.
  • Influential Points: Points that substantially change the regression line when removed.

Key Vocabulary

  • Response Variable: The variable we measure or predict (y).
  • Explanatory Variable: The variable we use to explain or predict the response (x).
  • Correlation Coefficient (r): A measure of the strength and direction of a linear relationship.
  • Least-Squares Regression: A method for finding the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals.
  • Residual: The difference between an observed value and the value predicted by the model.
  • Influential Point: A point whose removal changes the regression line substantially.
  • Lurking Variable: A variable not included in the study that can affect the relationship between x and y.
  • Extrapolation: Predicting outside the range of observed x-values — usually unreliable.

Core Skills

  • Analyze Data (Representations & Calculations): 3.A 3.B
  • Interpret Results (Justify Claims): 4.B
  • Interpret Results (Describe Distributions): 4.A

Assessments

  • Scatterplot interpretation exercises
  • Residual plot analysis
  • Calculator skills checks (LinReg, diagnostics r and r²)

Summative Assessments

  • Unit 5 Assessment (Week 22)
  • Regression FRQ practice
  • Monthly Exam (Apr 1–2)

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing correlation with causation, or r with slope.
  • Interpreting r² as "r squared is the correlation" instead of "proportion of variability explained".
  • Using a linear model when the residual plot shows a curved pattern.
  • Extrapolating beyond the range of the data.
  • Ignoring influential points and outliers when they change the model.
  • Interpreting the intercept when x = 0 is outside the data range.
