Course Overview and Cybersecurity Skills

CYBERSECURITY SKILLS (SPIRALED ACROSS UNITS)

The four cybersecurity skill categories are developed throughout the course using hands-on, scenario-based labs aligned with the NICE Workforce Framework.

  • 1 Analyze Risk
    Identify vulnerabilities, threats, and attacks; evaluate their likelihood and impact.
  • 2 Mitigate Risk
    Select and implement layered security controls across all domains.
  • 3 Detect Attacks
    Monitor systems, analyze logs, and classify cyberattacks from digital evidence.
  • 4 Collaborate
    Work with teammates and use AI tools responsibly on cybersecurity tasks.

SKILL CATEGORIES (SUB-SKILLS)

The College Board skill categories that students practice and master throughout the course:

  • 1.A Identify vulnerabilities, threats, and attacks
  • 1.B–1.D Determine exploitation methods; evaluate likelihood & impact; document risks
  • 2.A–2.D Identify controls; determine layered controls; evaluate strategies; implement mitigations
  • 3.A–3.D Identify detection methods; determine strategies; evaluate impact; analyze evidence
  • 4.A–4.D Team objectives; assign roles; use AI as tool; complete collaborative tasks

All hands-on labs follow the Defense-in-Depth Methodology — identify vulnerabilities, assess risks, implement layered controls, and verify detection capabilities.

Unit 1 - Introduction to Security (~10 periods)

Learning Objectives

  • Define cybersecurity and explain the importance of defense-in-depth strategies across multiple domains.
  • Identify and analyze social engineering tactics including intimidation, urgency, pretexting, authority, consensus, scarcity, and familiarity.
  • Recognize suspicious website logins, authentication attacks, and password-based vulnerabilities.
  • Evaluate security risks associated with public networks including evil twin attacks, jamming, and war driving.
  • Analyze AI-based cybersecurity threats including voice cloning, AI-crafted phishing, and automated attack tools.
  • Leverage AI tools responsibly for threat detection, log analysis, and defensive cybersecurity tasks.
  • Explain the fundamentals of multi-factor authentication (MFA) and its role in identity protection.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 10 class periods to complete (Weeks 1–2).
  • This unit contributes to foundational knowledge applied throughout the course.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 1.1–1.5
  • Cybersecurity Skills: Skills 1 (Analyze Risk) and 4 (Collaborate) are introduced in this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • What makes a cybersecurity attack successful beyond just technical vulnerabilities?
  • How can psychological manipulation (social engineering) bypass even the strongest technical defenses?
  • What are the risks of using public Wi-Fi, and how can they be mitigated?
  • How is artificial intelligence changing both cyber attacks and cyber defense?
  • Why is authentication the first line of defense in cybersecurity?
  • What role does human behavior play in organizational security?

Key Concepts

  • Social Engineering: Psychological manipulation to trick users into making security mistakes; tactics include intimidation, urgency, pretexting, authority, consensus, scarcity, familiarity.
  • Authentication Factors: Something you know (password), something you have (token), something you are (biometrics), somewhere you are (location), something you do (behavior).
  • Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Requires two or more authentication factors; significantly reduces account compromise risk.
  • Public Network Risks: Evil twin (rogue AP), jamming (signal interference), war driving (searching for open networks).
  • AI Cybersecurity Threats: Voice cloning, AI-crafted phishing emails, automated vulnerability scanning, adversarial AI.
  • AI Cybersecurity Defense: Threat detection algorithms, automated log analysis, behavioral anomaly detection, AI-assisted incident response.

Key Vocabulary

  • Social Engineering: The psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information.
  • Phishing: Fraudulent communication (email, text, call) pretending to be from a reputable source to steal data or install malware.
  • Pretexting: A form of social engineering where an attacker creates a fabricated scenario (pretext) to steal information or gain access.
  • Evil Twin: A rogue Wi-Fi access point that appears legitimate but is set up to eavesdrop on wireless communications.
  • Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): A security system requiring more than one method of authentication from independent categories of credentials.
  • Password Attack: Methods to obtain or crack passwords: brute force, dictionary attacks, credential stuffing, password spraying.
  • AI Voice Cloning: Using artificial intelligence to replicate a person's voice for impersonation attacks.

Unit 2 - Securing Spaces (~21 periods)

Learning Objectives

  • Classify adversary types: script kiddies, hacktivists, insiders, cyberterrorists, criminal organizations.
  • Explain the phases of a cyberattack: reconnaissance, initial access, persistence, lateral movement, action, evasion.
  • Conduct risk assessments using the risk formula: Risk = Likelihood × Impact.
  • Apply the CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) to evaluate security controls.
  • Identify and categorize physical vulnerabilities and attacks: tailgating, piggybacking, dumpster diving, shoulder surfing.
  • Implement physical security controls: locks, bollards, access control systems, visitor management.
  • Deploy detection methods: CCTV, motion sensors, alarm systems, security guards.
  • Design layered physical security using defense-in-depth principles.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 21 class periods to complete (Weeks 3–7).
  • Weeks 4–5 lose days to Mid-Autumn Festival (Sep 25–27) and National Day (Oct 1–7); Monthly Exam (Oct 8–10) replaces 3 days.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 2.1–2.4
  • Cybersecurity Skills: Skills 1 (Analyze Risk), 2 (Mitigate Risk), and 3 (Detect Attacks) are developed in this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • Who would want to attack an organization, and what motivates them?
  • How do attackers move from initial access to achieving their objectives?
  • How do you quantify and prioritize security risks in a meaningful way?
  • What are the trade-offs between security, usability, and cost when implementing physical controls?
  • How does defense-in-depth apply to physical security spaces?
  • What is the difference between prevention and detection, and why are both necessary?

Key Concepts

  • Adversary Types: Script kiddies (low skill), hacktivists (ideology), insiders (trusted access), cyberterrorists (disruption), criminal orgs (profit).
  • Cyberattack Phases: Recon → initial access → persistence → lateral movement → action (exfiltration/damage) → evasion.
  • Risk Assessment: Risk = Likelihood × Impact; qualitative (low/medium/high) vs. quantitative (dollar amounts).
  • CIA Triad: Confidentiality (prevent unauthorized disclosure), Integrity (prevent unauthorized modification), Availability (ensure access when needed).
  • Security Controls: Physical (locks, fences), Technical (alarms, CCTV), Managerial (policies, training).
  • Defense-in-Depth: Layered security strategy; if one control fails, others provide backup protection.

Key Vocabulary

  • Risk Management: The process of identifying, assessing, and prioritizing risks followed by coordinated application of resources to minimize impact.
  • Vulnerability: A weakness in a system that can be exploited by a threat to gain unauthorized access or cause damage.
  • Threat: A potential cause of an unwanted incident that may result in harm to a system or organization.
  • Tailgating: Following an authorized person into a restricted area without proper authentication.
  • Defense-in-Depth: A security strategy that uses multiple layers of controls to protect assets; no single point of failure.
  • Physical Control: Security measures that use physical barriers to prevent or detect unauthorized access (locks, fences, guards).
  • Technical Control: Security measures that use technology to prevent or detect unauthorized access (alarms, CCTV, access cards).

Unit 3 - Securing Networks (~26 periods)

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the OSI model and TCP/IP protocol suite fundamentals.
  • Identify network vulnerabilities and attacks: packet sniffing, ARP spoofing, evil twin (network layer), MITM, DNS spoofing/poisoning, DDoS, port scanning, OSINT reconnaissance.
  • Implement managerial controls for network security: network security policies, WPA3, WPA2-Enterprise, MAC filtering, VPN for clients.
  • Design network segmentation using VLANs, subnets, DMZ, and network zones to limit lateral movement.
  • Configure and analyze firewall rules: packet-filtering, stateful, NGFW; ACL rule logic and ordering.
  • Deploy detection systems: IDS vs. IPS; signature vs. anomaly detection; SIEM overview.
  • Analyze network traffic using Wireshark and log analysis to identify attack indicators.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 26 class periods to complete (Weeks 8–13).
  • Midterm Exam Week (Nov 6–11) and Autumn Outing (Nov 12) interrupt. Available teaching days: ~30; 26 periods of instruction, 4 days for lab, review, and assessment.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 3.1–3.5
  • Cybersecurity Skills: Skills 1, 2, and 3 are heavily developed in this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • How does data move across networks, and where are the vulnerabilities?
  • What is the difference between network monitoring and network protection, and why are both needed?
  • How does network segmentation limit the impact of a successful breach?
  • How do firewall rules work, and what happens when rule order is incorrect?
  • What is the difference between an IDS and an IPS, and when should each be used?
  • How can you identify a network attack from log data and traffic patterns?

Key Concepts

  • OSI Model: 7-layer network model (Physical to Application); TCP/IP condenses to 4 layers. Understanding layers helps identify where attacks occur.
  • MITM Attack: Man-in-the-Middle; attacker intercepts and potentially alters communication between two parties without their knowledge.
  • DNS Spoofing: Attacker corrupts DNS cache or responds to queries with false IP addresses, redirecting traffic to malicious sites.
  • DDoS: Distributed Denial of Service; overwhelming a target with traffic from multiple sources to make it unavailable.
  • VLAN/Subnet/DMZ: Virtual LANs segment broadcast domains; subnets partition IP networks; DMZ provides isolated zone for public-facing services.
  • Firewall Types: Packet-filtering (layer 3/4 rules), Stateful (track connections), NGFW (layer 7, application awareness).
  • IDS/IPS: Intrusion Detection System (alerts only) vs. Intrusion Prevention System (blocks automatically).

Key Vocabulary

  • Packet Sniffing: Capturing data packets as they travel across a network; can reveal sensitive information if traffic is unencrypted.
  • ARP Spoofing: Attack that links an attacker's MAC address to a legitimate IP address, enabling MITM attacks on a local network.
  • Network Segmentation: Dividing a network into smaller parts (segments) to improve security and performance; limits lateral movement.
  • DMZ (Demilitarized Zone): A physical or logical subnetwork that contains an organization's external-facing services, isolated from the internal network.
  • ACL (Access Control List): A list of rules that determines which traffic is allowed or denied through a network device like a firewall or router.
  • SIEM (Security Information and Event Management): A solution that aggregates and analyzes log data from multiple sources to detect threats.
  • Wireshark: A network protocol analyzer used to capture and inspect network traffic in real-time.

Unit 4 - Securing Devices (~23 periods)

Learning Objectives

  • Classify malware types: virus, worm, ransomware, spyware, RAT, rootkit; understand OS and firmware vulnerabilities.
  • Explain zero-day vulnerabilities and privilege escalation techniques.
  • Implement authentication strategies: password policies, biometrics, smart cards, MFA factor types, SSO and OAuth overview.
  • Apply device protection measures: patch management workflow, antimalware and EDR, OS hardening (least privilege, disable unused services), full-disk encryption.
  • Deploy mobile device management (MDM) and bring-your-own-device (BYOD) policies.
  • Analyze endpoint event logs and SIEM integration to detect behavioral IoCs: unusual processes, outbound connections.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 23 class periods to complete (Weeks 13–17).
  • Unit 4 begins in the final days of Week 13 (after Unit 3 Assessment). Monthly Exam (Dec 14–18) provides ~3 teaching days; Dec 28–31 is 4 days (New Year Jan 1).
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 4.1–4.4
  • Cybersecurity Skills: Skills 2 (Mitigate Risk) and 3 (Detect Attacks) are the focus of this unit.

Guiding Questions

  • What is the difference between viruses, worms, and ransomware, and how do their impacts differ?
  • Why are zero-day vulnerabilities particularly dangerous, and how should organizations prepare?
  • What makes a strong authentication system beyond just complex passwords?
  • How does the principle of least privilege reduce security risk?
  • What are the security implications of employees using personal devices for work (BYOD)?
  • How can endpoint logs reveal an ongoing attack that went undetected by perimeter defenses?

Key Concepts

  • Malware Taxonomy: Virus (attaches to files), Worm (self-replicates), Ransomware (encrypts for ransom), Spyware (steals info), RAT (remote access), Rootkit (hides presence).
  • Zero-Day: A vulnerability unknown to the software vendor; no patch exists; highly valuable to attackers.
  • Privilege Escalation: Gaining higher-level permissions than intended; vertical (user→admin) or horizontal (user→another user).
  • MFA Factors: Knowledge (password), Possession (token), Inherence (biometric), Location, Behavior.
  • OS Hardening: Disabling unused services, removing unnecessary software, applying least privilege, configuring firewalls.
  • EDR (Endpoint Detection & Response): Advanced endpoint protection that records and analyzes endpoint activities to detect threats.

Key Vocabulary

  • Ransomware: Malware that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment (ransom) to restore access.
  • Rootkit: Malware designed to gain administrative-level access and hide its presence from users and system processes.
  • Patch Management: The process of managing software updates (patches) to fix security vulnerabilities and improve functionality.
  • Full-Disk Encryption (FDE): Encryption that protects all data on a storage device; data is unreadable without the decryption key.
  • MDM (Mobile Device Management): Software that allows IT administrators to control, secure, and enforce policies on mobile devices used in an organization.
  • BYOD (Bring Your Own Device): Policy allowing employees to use personal devices for work purposes; requires security controls to mitigate risk.
  • Behavioral IoC: Indicators of Compromise based on unusual behavior patterns (e.g., unusual process execution, unexpected outbound connections).

Unit 5 - Securing Applications and Data (~22 periods)

Learning Objectives

  • Identify application vulnerabilities: SQL injection, XSS, CSRF; understand OWASP Top 10.
  • Explain how unsanitized input creates security risks in web applications.
  • Implement managerial controls and access controls: SDLC security integration, DAC, MAC, RBAC, least privilege, separation of duties.
  • Apply symmetric cryptography: AES, hashing (SHA-256), salting for password storage.
  • Apply asymmetric cryptography: RSA, digital signatures, PKI, certificates, TLS/SSL.
  • Protect applications using secure coding: prepared statements, input validation and sanitization, WAF, HTTPS enforcement, secure session management.
  • Detect application attacks using application log patterns for SQL injection and XSS, rate limiting, and anomaly detection rules.

Curriculum Standards

  • The unit will take approximately 22 class periods to complete (Weeks 17–20, Mar 1–31).
  • Unit 5 is compressed from the CED's ~30 periods to ~22, which is feasible because students enter with strong foundations from Units 1–4.
  • Spring Outing (Mar 24–26) removes 3 days. Monthly Exam Week (Mar 29–Apr 2) provides ~3 teaching days alongside the exam.
  • College Board CED Topics covered: 5.1–5.6
  • Cybersecurity Skills: All four skills are integrated in this culminating unit.

Guiding Questions

  • How can unsanitized user input lead to complete server compromise?
  • What is the difference between symmetric and asymmetric cryptography, and when should each be used?
  • How do access control models (DAC, MAC, RBAC) differ in their approach to authorization?
  • Why is input validation the first line of defense for web application security?
  • How does TLS/SSL secure communication, and what role do digital certificates play?
  • What patterns in application logs indicate an ongoing SQL injection or XSS attack?

Key Concepts

  • OWASP Top 10: Standard awareness document listing the 10 most critical web application security risks; updated regularly.
  • SQL Injection: Attack that inserts malicious SQL code into a query; can bypass authentication, extract, or modify data.
  • XSS (Cross-Site Scripting): Attack that injects malicious scripts into trusted websites; executed in victims' browsers.
  • CSRF (Cross-Site Request Forgery): Attack that tricks a user's browser into executing unwanted actions on a trusted site where they're authenticated.
  • Symmetric Encryption: Same key for encryption and decryption (AES); fast but requires secure key distribution.
  • Asymmetric Encryption: Public/private key pair (RSA); solves key distribution problem; slower than symmetric.
  • Hashing: One-way function that produces a fixed-size output; used for password storage (with salt) and integrity verification.

Key Vocabulary

  • Prepared Statements: Parameterized SQL queries that separate SQL code from data; prevent SQL injection by treating user input as data, not executable code.
  • Input Validation: The process of verifying that user input meets expected criteria before processing; first line of defense against injection attacks.
  • Digital Signature: A cryptographic technique that verifies the authenticity and integrity of a message using asymmetric encryption.
  • PKI (Public Key Infrastructure): A framework for managing digital certificates and public-key encryption; enables secure electronic transfer.
  • WAF (Web Application Firewall): A firewall that filters, monitors, and blocks HTTP traffic to and from a web application; protects against web exploits.
  • Salting: Adding random data to passwords before hashing; prevents rainbow table attacks and ensures identical passwords have different hashes.
  • Rate Limiting: Controlling the number of requests a user or IP can make in a given time period; mitigates DDoS and brute-force attacks.

AP Exam Format

Section I: Multiple Choice

  • ~70 scenario-based questions
  • 120 minutes
  • 60% of total score

Section II: Free Response

  • 2 extended scenario analysis questions
  • 90 minutes
  • 40% of total score

Free-Response Expectations

Both FRQs present an authentic cybersecurity workplace scenario. Students must:

  • Identify vulnerabilities and classify their risk level
  • Recommend and justify layered security controls
  • Analyze configuration data or logs as evidence of an attack
  • Explain how detection methods work and evaluate their impact
