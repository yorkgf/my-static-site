The four legal structures — in the examples

Each structure maps onto one moment in the snack box story:

Lesson

Sole proprietorship — definition

A sole proprietorship is a business owned and run by one person.

  • The owner has full control over every decision
  • The owner keeps all the profits
  • The owner is personally liable for all business debts
  • Funding comes from the owner's own savings and loans
Key PointSimplest structure. No filings, no partners, no board. One person, one business.
Lesson

Sole proprietorship — in the examples

The snack box founder on the first Saturday:

  • One owner — nobody else has a claim
  • Full control — he decides the menu, the price, the route
- Keeps every dollar — profit = revenue minus cost, all his
  • Personal liability — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, he pays from his own pocket
  • Limited funding — growth depends on what he can save or borrow
Key PointFive hats, one head. CEO, marketer, product developer, financial manager, operations manager — all on the same Saturday.
Lesson

Partnership — definition

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who share control and profits.

  • Owners share control and share profits — usually by agreement
  • All partners are personally liable for business debts — even debts caused by the other partner
  • Funding is still limited — comes from the partners' savings and loans
  • No legal filings required to form (in most cases) — a handshake can do it
Exam NoteThe exam's favorite trap: liability is shared, not divided by who did the work. If the cousin drops the cooler, the founder still pays.
Lesson

Partnership — in the examples

The cousin joins the snack box:

  • Two owners — handshake agreement, no paperwork
  • Shared control — the cousin handles logistics, the founder handles the product
  • Shared profits — they split the cash box at the end of the day
  • Personal liability — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, both pay, regardless of whose hand was on the lid
  • Limited funding — still just two kids' savings
Exam NoteThe handshake divided the profits. It never mentioned the debts. Both are personally liable — the exam tests this exact point.
Lesson

LLC — definition

A limited liability company (LLC) is a business structure where owners retain control and keep profits, but their personal assets are shielded from business debts.

  • Owners keep control — no board, no shareholders
  • Liability is shielded — business debts belong to the business, not to the owners personally
  • Funding is still limited — no shares to sell
  • Requires legal filings to form
Key PointThe LLC draws a wall between the business and the owner's personal money. The phone-screen question stops here.
Lesson

LLC — in the examples

The phone-screen question pushes the snack box toward an LLC:

  • Same two owners — but now with a legal filing
  • Same control — they still make every decision together
  • Shielded liability — if the cooler lid breaks a phone, the business pays, not their personal savings
  • Limited funding — they can't sell shares, so growth still depends on savings and loans
Key PointThe LLC answers the parent's question: "Which one of you pays?" → The business does. That's the wall the filing builds.
Lesson

Corporation — definition

A corporation is a business owned by shareholders who elect a board of directors to make major decisions.

  • Owners cede control to shareholders and an elected board
  • Liability is shielded — same wall as the LLC
  • Greatest access to funding — the corporation can sell shares to raise money
  • The most complex and expensive structure to form and maintain
Key PointThe trade: you give up control, and in exchange you get the most funding and the strongest liability shield.
Lesson

Corporation — in the examples

If the snack box needed to expand to 40 leagues at once:

  • Shareholders buy in — each one owns a piece of the business
  • A board is elected — the founder no longer makes every call alone
  • Selling shares raises the money for 40 new vans and 40 new cooler sets
  • Shielded liability — same wall as the LLC
Key PointThe founder traded control for scale. He can't decide the menu alone anymore — but the business can reach leagues he could never fund by himself.
Lesson

Tradeoff line 1 — control vs. funding

Key PointControl and funding pull in opposite directions.

Owners of a sole proprietorship, partnership, or LLC retain control over decision making and keep the profits — but the price is that growth may be limited, because these structures have less access to funding.

A corporation flips the trade: its owners cede control to shareholders and an elected board, and in exchange the corporation gains greater access to funding and room to grow, with the company itself controlling the profits.

Lesson

Tradeoff line 2 — liability — definition

Key PointWho pays when something goes wrong?

Sole proprietors and partners are personally liable for all business debts — a broken screen, an unpaid loan, or a lawsuit can reach into their own pockets.

Organizing as an LLC lets owners avoid that personal exposure — the business's debts belong to the business. A corporation draws the same wall.

Lesson

Tradeoff line 2 — liability — in the examples

The phone-screen question is the test:

  • Sole proprietorship: the founder pays from his own savings
  • Partnership: both the founder and the cousin pay — regardless of whose hand was on the lid
  • LLC: the business pays — the owners' personal savings are untouched
  • Corporation: same wall — the business pays
Key PointThe parent's question — "which one of you pays?" — has a different answer in every structure. That's why the structure matters.
Lesson

The 3-question identification method

Key PointEvery identification question in this topic surrenders to these three questions, in order:
  1. How many owners — one or several?
  2. Who pays the business's debts — the owners personally, or the business alone?
  3. How does growth get funded — from savings and loans, or by selling shares?
Answer patternStructure
One owner, personal liability, self-fundedSole proprietorship
Several owners, personal liabilityPartnership
Owners shielded, no shareholders/boardLLC
Shareholders, a board, stock fundingCorporation
Lesson

The 3-question method — in the examples

Run the snack box through the three questions at each stage:

Stage 1 — one kid, one Saturday:

Key PointHow many owners? One. Who pays debts? He does — personally. How funded? His savings. → Sole proprietorship.

Stage 2 — the cousin joins:

Key PointHow many owners? Two. Who pays debts? Both — personally. How funded? Their savings. → Partnership.

Stage 3 — after the filing:

Key PointHow many owners? Two. Who pays debts? The business — liability shielded. How funded? Still their savings. → LLC.

Stage 4 — 40-league expansion:

Key PointHow many owners? Shareholders. Who pays debts? The business — liability shielded. How funded? Selling shares. → Corporation.
Lesson

Exam tip — start with question 1

Key PointStart with the first question (how many owners?) and you can eliminate half the options immediately.
  • One owner → sole proprietorship or (rarely) an LLC with one member
  • Two or more owners, personal liability → partnership
  • Two or more owners, shielded, no board → LLC
  • Shareholders and a board → corporation
Key PointThe first question is the fastest filter. Use it before reading the full scenario.

Roles and responsibilities

Structure says who owns the business. Roles say who does the work.
Lesson

One owner, every hat — definition

In a sole proprietorship, one person does all of it — five roles at once:

RoleWhat it means
Chief executive officerSetting the direction, making the big calls
MarketerGetting the word out, building the brand
Product developerDesigning and improving what's sold
Financial managerCounting the money, tracking costs
Operations managerRunning the day-to-day — logistics, delivery, execution
Key PointThe hats compete for hours — a Saturday spent under the operations hat leaves the marketing hat unworn all week.
Lesson

One owner, every hat — in the examples

The snack box founder on a single Saturday:

RoleWhat he does
CEODecides to add fruit cups after the families' feedback

Financial managerCounts the cash box, calculates the weekend profit
Operations managerLoads the cooler, drives to the field, hands off at halftime
Key PointFive hats, one head. Every minute under one hat is a minute stolen from another. That's why partners matter.
Lesson

Partners divide the roles — definition

Partners carry the same full responsibility, but typically divide the roles according to each partner's strengths and interests.

  • One partner might handle operations and logistics — routes, schedules, handoffs
  • The other might handle product, pricing, and money — the menu, the cash box, the group chat
  • The division isn't about hierarchy — it's about fit
Key PointA partnership where both partners fight over the same hat wastes one of its two advantages.
Lesson

Key PointThe cousin loves logistics — routes, coolers, handoffs. The founder keeps the product, the pricing, and the money. Each partner wears the hats that fit.
Exam NoteThe exam tests this as "dividing roles by strengths and interests" — not by seniority or ownership percentage.

Growing into departments — in the examples

The snack box at 40-league scale:

Lesson

Organizational structure — definition

Organizational structure
the arrangement of roles, responsibility, authority, and communication. Drawing it as a chart (an org chart) makes the reporting lines readable at a glance.

Watch one decision travel the chart:

  1. An operations manager pitches opening league 41
  2. The CEO weighs it against strategy and says yes
  3. At the next board meeting the CEO answers to the directors and shareholders for how the expansion performed
Key PointAuthority flows down; accountability flows up. That's the structure doing its job.
Lesson

Organizational structure — in the examples

The snack box at league 41:

  • Operations manager: "League 41 needs a team. Here's the cost."
  • CEO (the founder): "Does it fit the expansion plan? Can we staff it?"
  • Board meeting: "How did league 41 perform against the budget?"
Key PointThe org chart isn't a drawing — it's the path every decision follows. Authority goes down, accountability comes back up.

The swap pair: Accounting vs. Finance — definition

Exam NoteAccounting and Finance are the swap pair the exam loves.
AccountingFinance
DirectionRecords and reports money that already movedWorks forward
What it doesTracks, prepares statements, monitors healthSecures funds, manages them, recommends what's next
LooksBackwardForward
Key PointRecords look backward, funding looks forward. Pin this difference now.
Lesson

The swap pair: Accounting vs. Finance — in the examples

The snack box cash box:

- Accounting = the cash box itself — every dollar in, every dollar out, the weekend totals on a sheet of paper. Looking backward at what already happened.
- Finance = the decision to fund league 41 — do we have enough cash? Should we borrow? Is the return worth it? Looking forward at what should happen next.
Key PointA scenario about preparing statements belongs to accounting — even when the word "finance" appears in it.
Key PointThe cash box is accounting. The decision to spend from it is finance.
Lesson

Accounting vs. Finance — the exam trick

The exam writes scenarios that use the word "financial" to pull you toward the wrong answer:

  • "The department that prepares the financial statements" → Accounting (backward — records)
  • "The department that secures funds for expansion" → Finance (forward — funding)
Exam Note"Financial" is unreliable as a signal. The direction test works every time: backward = accounting, forward = finance.

Outsourcing

Buy the function, or build it.
Lesson

Outsourcing — definition

Outsourcing
hiring another business to handle a function instead of doing it internally.

A business may outsource a function when doing so is likely to increase efficiency or reduce costs.

Classic triggers:

  1. The business lacks employees with the specific skills
  2. Existing labor costs run high
Key PointThe outsourcing decision in one sentence: "buy the function, or build it."
Lesson

Outsourcing — in the examples

The snack box meets the first trigger:

  • Pre-orders outgrow the group chat — families want a real ordering system
  • Neither the founder nor the cousin can code
  • They pay someone to build a simple order page
Key PointHiring the skill for a fee beat learning it for a season. The function moved outside because the skill didn't exist inside.
Lesson

Outsourcing — the second trigger

A business might also outsource when labor costs run high:

  • A company in a high-wage city hires an outside firm to run customer support from a lower-cost region
  • A manufacturer contracts packaging to a third party because building a packaging line is too expensive
Key PointTwo triggers, same decision: the function moves outside when building it in-house costs more — in money, time, or both.
Lesson

Outsourcing vs. core competency

Key PointOutsourcing is the opposite of developing a core competency.
Core competencyOutsourcing
DirectionDevelop a function in-houseMove a function outside
WhyThe function is a source of competitive advantageThe function is a cost to manage
ExampleThe snack box's halftime handoff (a skill they built)The order page (a skill they bought)
Exam NoteIf a function is your edge, keep it. If a function is a cost, buy it. The exam tests this distinction.

Discussion scenario

A small bakery makes everything in-house. Demand for wedding cakes is growing, but the bakery has no one trained in cake decorating. The owner considers two options:

  1. Hire and train a cake decorator (build the skill)
  2. Contract a freelance cake decorator for each order (buy the skill)
Key PointPair up. 2 minutes.
  1. What is the outsourcing decision here — buy or build?
  2. What are the tradeoffs of each option?
  3. Which option would you recommend, and why?
Lesson

Discussion — key points

Build (hire and train):

  • The skill stays in-house — becomes a potential core competency
  • Higher upfront cost (salary, training time)
  • Long-term control over quality

Buy (contract):

  • Lower upfront cost — pay per order
  • The skill stays outside — no core competency built
  • Less control over quality and availability
Key PointThe outsourcing decision depends on whether the function is a cost to manage or an edge to build. Wedding cakes might be the bakery's future — or just a seasonal spike.

Practice Question
Q1Liability for the ruined jacket

Aiko and Jordan formed Tumble Route with a spoken agreement and no legal filings. A customer's jacket was ruined. Which best describes who is liable for the $800 claim?

AOnly Aiko, because she manages the machines
BOnly Jordan, because he handles the accounts
CThe business alone — neither Aiko nor Jordan is personally liable
DBoth Aiko and Jordan are personally liable

Pause. Ask: is this a sole proprietorship, a partnership, an LLC, or a corporation? What does the structure say about liability?

-->

Answer D

Why

A spoken agreement with no legal filings = a partnership. In a partnership, all partners are personally liable for business debts — regardless of whose area the damage occurred in.

A — the exam's favorite partnership trap. Liability is shared, not divided by who manages the machines.** Aiko runs the machines, but Jordan is still liable.
B — same trap in reverse. Jordan handles accounts, but the jacket wasn't damaged by accounting. It doesn't matter.** Both partners are liable.
C — borrows the LLC/corporation rule. No filings = no liability shield. The business is** the partners.
Key PointPartnership liability is shared and personal — not divided by who did the work.
Practice Question
Q2Classify the role split

Aiko manages routes, machines, and the schedule. Jordan handles pricing, advertising, and accounts. This arrangement best illustrates which concept?

APartners dividing roles by strengths and interests
BDepartments forming within an organizational structure
COutsourcing specialized functions to external firms
DA corporation electing a board of directors
Answer A

Why

Two partners with a spoken agreement, each handling the areas that match their skills — logistics for Aiko, business operations for Jordan. This is partners dividing roles by strengths and interests.

B — departments form when a business grows large enough to need specialized teams with managers. Two partners splitting work is not departments — scale is the tell.** If the scenario said "Tumble Route has 50 employees organized into teams," then departments would apply.
Key PointPartners dividing roles ≠ departments. The difference is scale and specialization.
Practice Question
Q3Tradeoff of incorporating

If Tumble Route incorporates and sells shares to finance 20 new vans, which best describes the tradeoff?

AThe business loses its liability shield but gains funding
BThe partners gain more personal control over daily decisions
CThe owners cede control to shareholders and a board in exchange for greater access to funding
DThe business can no longer grow because shareholders limit decision making
Answer C

Why

Incorporating means selling shares — shareholders elect a board, and the founders cede control over major decisions. In exchange, the corporation gains the greatest access to funding — exactly what 20 new vans require.

A — flips the liability direction. Incorporating keeps** the liability shield (same as LLC). The trade is control for funding, not liability for funding.
D — reverses the relationship. Shareholders enable growth by providing funding — they don't limit it. The limitation is on the founders' control**, not on the business's ability to grow.
Key PointControl and funding pull in opposite directions. Incorporating shrinks personal liability, cedes control, and unlocks funding.

A software company hires an outside firm to run its customer-support phone lines because support wages in its city are unusually high. Which best describes this decision?

BThe company has converted the support staff into internal stakeholders
