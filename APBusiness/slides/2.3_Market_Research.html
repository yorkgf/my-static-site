Lesson deck — concepts + practice

Running example continues: Theo / Trued Cycles.

Lesson

Opening scenario

A buyer holds up his phone showing a $75 bike online and asks why Theo charges $95. Theo points at the orange inspection tag. The buyer shrugs and walks.

Key PointThat night Theo writes: "An identical bike with a documented inspection tag sells for twenty dollars more, and faster."

Today that's a guess. This topic turns guesses into evidence.

Lesson

What is market research?

Market research
the process of collecting and analyzing information to guide business decisions about customers, competitors, and the market.
Key PointIt replaces "I think" with "the data shows."
  • Every business decision — pricing, product design, targeting — improves when it rests on evidence instead of instinct.
Lesson

EK 2.3.A.1 — Why it matters

Without research, a business is guessing:

GuessEvidence
"People want this product"Survey: 72% of target customers say they'd buy
"Our price is right"Competitor scan: median price is $89, ours is $95
"Customers love us"NPS score: 62 (promoters minus detractors)
Key PointResearch turns assumptions into testable claims.
Lesson

Quantitative vs. qualitative data

🔢 Numbers

Quantitative

Data expressed in numbers. Answers "how many?" "how much?" "how often?"

Example: 72% of 200 surveyed would buy.

💬 Words & meaning

Qualitative

Data expressed in words, images, observations. Answers "why?" "how do you feel?"

Example: "I like the color but the seat is too hard."

Key PointQuant = count it. Qual = understand it.
Lesson

Quantitative vs. qualitative — Theo examples

Quantitative:

  • 60 marketplace listings scraped — median price $82
- 7 out of 9 tagged bikes sold in one Saturday = 78%

Qualitative:

  • 12 one-on-one interviews → "The tag makes me trust it's not stolen"
  • A buyer saying "I'd pay $10 more if I knew it was safe"
Exam NoteThe numbers tell you WHAT happened. The words tell you WHY.
Lesson

Desirable / Feasible / Viable

CriterionQuestion it answersTheo example
DesirableDoes the customer actually want this? (problem-solution fit)Do buyers value the inspection tag enough to pay more?
FeasibleCan we actually build / deliver it? (resources, tech, time)Can Theo inspect and tag 15+ bikes per week with current tools?
ViableIs there a profitable path? (revenue > cost, long-term)Does the $20 premium cover the cost of inspection and tagging?
Key PointAll three must pass. A desirable product you can't build is a dream. A feasible product nobody wants is a waste.
Lesson

Desirable / Feasible / Viable — the framework

Think of it as three gates:

  1. Desirable — "Do they want it?" → research with customers
  2. Feasible — "Can we do it?" → audit your resources and capacity
  3. Viable — "Does the math work?" → model revenue vs. cost
Key PointGate order matters: desirability first (no point building something nobody wants), then feasibility, then viability.
Lesson

Desirability — problem-solution fit

Desirable
the product solves a real customer problem and the customer values the solution enough to choose it over alternatives.
  • Test with customer interviews, surveys, observation
  • Look for: willingness to pay, switching behavior, emotional response
Key PointTheo's desirability question: "Will buyers pay $95 for a bike with an inspection tag when a $75 bike without one is available?"
Lesson

Feasibility — resources, tech, time

Feasible
the business has (or can acquire) the resources, technology, and time to deliver the product.
  • Test with internal audits, capacity analysis, supplier checks
  • Look for: bottlenecks, skill gaps, supply constraints
Key PointTheo's feasibility question: "Can I inspect, tag, and photograph 15 bikes per week with one helper and a Saturday schedule?"
Lesson

Viability — profitable path

Viable
the business model produces revenue greater than cost over time, creating a sustainable path.
  • Test with financial modeling, break-even analysis, pricing research
  • Look for: margins, customer acquisition cost, repeat purchase rate
Key PointTheo's viability question: "Does the $20 premium on tagged bikes cover inspection labor, tag materials, and still leave profit?"
Lesson

Real-world case — New Coke (1985)

Coca-Cola in the 1980s: owned 60% of the cola market after WWII → less than 24% by 1983.

Pepsi Challenge: blindfolded taste tests → people overwhelmingly chose Pepsi.

Coca-Cola's response: Project Kansas — create a sweeter Coke to beat Pepsi.

Key PointThey spent $12 million (today's dollars) testing with 200,000 people. New Coke won every taste test. They launched it.

Three months later, they pulled it.

Lesson

New Coke — what the research showed

Methods used:

  • Sampling surveys (quantitative): 200,000 people tasted New Coke vs Classic vs Pepsi
  • Focus groups (qualitative): discussed the new flavor

Results:

  • New Coke "overwhelmingly beat both Pepsi and Coke Classic"
  • One bottling partner threatened to sue if they didn't release it
Key PointThe data said launch it. The data was right about taste — and wrong about everything else.
Lesson

New Coke — where the research failed

What they measuredWhat they MISSED
Taste preference (sip test)Emotional attachment to the original brand
"Do you prefer A or B?""How would you feel if Classic disappeared forever?"
Small samples in a controlled settingReal-world full-can drinking experience
Quantitative data (how many prefer it)Qualitative data on why people love Coke
Key PointThe research measured the product. It didn't measure the brand.
Lesson

New Coke — three research mistakes

1. Sample design flaw (EK 2.3.C.8)

  • "They only gave out a small amount — little samples didn't reflect how people drank Coke in real life"
  • A sip ≠ a full can in your kitchen

2. Missing the key variable

  • Focus groups asked about taste but NOT about emotional attachment
  • "We are not selling a soft drink. We were selling a little tiny piece of people's lives."

3. Confirmation bias (EK 2.3.C.8)

  • CEO Goizueta's mind was already made up: "it was new Coke or no Coke"
  • He ignored data that contradicted his belief
  • Not neutral → not honest research
Lesson

New Coke — the lesson

8,000 angry calls per day to Coca-Cola headquarters.

Demonstrations. Letter-writing campaigns. Media frenzy.

Coca-Cola brought back the original as "Coca-Cola Classic" within 3 months.

Key PointResearch ≠ guaranteed prediction. 200,000 data points and $12 million said "launch." The market said "no."

A former Coca-Cola executive: "We did not know what we were selling. We are not selling a soft drink. We were selling a little tiny piece of people's lives."

Lesson

Research after launch

Research does not stop when the product launches. Post-launch research:

PurposeWhat it doesTheo example
Track changesMonitor shifts in customer preferences or market conditionsBuyer tastes shift from mountain bikes to city commuters
Spot openingsIdentify new market opportunities or unmet needsParents start asking for kid-sized bikes with the same tag
Build strategiesUse data to adjust pricing, promotion, or positioningRaise tagged-bike price to $100 after confirming demand holds
Key PointA business that stops researching after launch is guessing again within months.
Lesson

EK 2.3.A.4 — research is ongoing

Key PointResearch is not a one-time event — it's a continuous cycle.

The market moves. Customers change. Competitors react. What was true in January may be false by June.

  • Pre-launch: test desirability, feasibility, viability
  • Post-launch: track, spot, adjust
  • Ongoing: the cycle never ends

LO 2.3.B — Secondary-Source Research

What's already out there — and how to use it cheaply.
Lesson

What is secondary-source research?

Secondary-source research = collecting and analyzing data that already exists — gathered by someone else for a different purpose.

Sources include:

TypeExamples
GovernmentCensus data, labor statistics, trade reports
CommercialIndustry reports, market research firms (Statista, IBISWorld)
AcademicUniversity studies, published journals, working papers
InternalThe company's own past sales records, customer databases
Key PointKey advantage: it's faster and cheaper than collecting new data yourself.
Lesson

EK 2.3.B.2 — what secondary research reveals

Secondary research helps a business understand the landscape before investing in primary research:

What it revealsHow Theo uses it
PESTEL factorsLocal regulations on reselling used bikes (legal), e-bike trend (technological)
Market sizeHow many used bikes sell per month in his city
TrendsGrowing demand for inspected/refurbished bikes
Customer segmentsCommuters vs. recreational riders vs. parents buying for kids
Competitor analysisWhat other sellers charge, how they position, what they lack
Exam NoteSecondary research = cheap reconnaissance. It tells you what you're walking into.
Lesson

EK 2.3.B.3 — secondary is cost-effective

Key PointSecondary research is the cost-effective first step — it saves time and money by using data that already exists.
  • Pro: fast, cheap, broad coverage
  • Con: may not answer your specific question; data may be outdated; you didn't control how it was collected
Key PointAlways start with secondary. Only move to primary when secondary leaves gaps.
Lesson

Theo's secondary move

Theo sweeps 60 marketplace listings and records three fields per listing:

FieldWhat he finds
PriceRange: $55–$110; median: $82
Days listedCheaper bikes sit longer; tagged bikes sell faster
Inspection documented?Only 8 of 60 mention any inspection
Key PointWithout spending a dollar on new data, Theo now knows: most competitors don't document inspections, and there may be a price gap he can fill.
Lesson

Your turn — secondary sources

What secondary sources could you use to research your own business idea (or a school project)?

Consider:

  • Industry reports (what does your school/library give access to?)

LO 2.3.C — Primary-Source Research

Generating new data from real customers.
Lesson

What is primary-source research?

Primary-source research = collecting new data directly from customers, prospects, or the market — data that did not exist before you gathered it.
Key PointSecondary = what's already out there. Primary = what you go get yourself.
  • Surveys, interviews, focus groups, experiments, observations
  • More expensive and time-consuming than secondary
  • But it answers your specific question with your specific audience
Lesson

Business hypothesis

Key PointBusiness hypothesis = an assumption about a customer, product, or market, stated so it can be tested.
ComponentExample (Theo)
AssumptionBuyers will pay more for a bike with a documented inspection tag
Testable form"If I add an inspection tag, at least 60% of bikes will sell within one week at $95"
Key PointA hypothesis is not a wish. It must specify what you expect and how you'll know if you're right or wrong.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.2 — why hypotheses matter

Without a hypothesis, research is just collecting data with no direction.

  • A hypothesis focuses your research: you know what to measure
  • It makes results interpretable: you can say "supported" or "not supported"
  • It prevents post-hoc rationalization: deciding after the fact what the data "means"
Key PointWrite the hypothesis before you collect the data. Not after.
Lesson

Research methods menu

MethodType of dataBest forTheo example
SurveyQuantitativeMeasuring opinions, preferences across a large groupOnline survey: "Would you pay more for an inspected bike?"
Focus groupQualitativeExploring attitudes, generating ideas in a group setting6 buyers discuss what "inspection" means to them
InterviewQualitativeDeep, one-on-one understanding of motivations12 follow-up interviews after the A/B test
Experiment / ObservationBehavioralWatching what people actually do (not just what they say)Count how many people pick up a tagged vs. untagged bike
A/B testingBehavioral (real-world)Comparing two versions to see which performs better9 tagged vs. 8 untagged bikes over two Saturdays
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.3 — match the method to the data

Exam NoteThe hypothesis tells you what data you need. The method must deliver that type of data.
If your hypothesis asks…You need…Use…
"What do customers prefer?"Quantitative preference dataSurvey
"Why do customers feel that way?"Qualitative depthInterview or focus group
"Will customers actually buy?"Behavioral dataExperiment, observation, A/B test
Key PointDon't use a survey to answer a "why" question. Don't use a focus group to count behavior.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.4 — surveys

Survey
a set of questions administered to a sample of respondents to collect quantitative (or sometimes qualitative) data.
  • Can reach large numbers quickly (online tools, paper forms)
  • Best for: preferences, satisfaction, demographics, likelihood to buy
  • Weakness: people may not answer honestly (social desirability bias)
Exam NoteTheo's survey: "On a scale of 1–5, how important is a documented inspection when buying a used bike?" — sent to 200 local commuters.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.5 — focus groups and interviews

Focus group
a moderated discussion with 6–10 participants to explore attitudes and generate qualitative insights.
Interview = a one-on-one conversation to get deep, detailed qualitative responses.
  • Best for: understanding motivations, feelings, reasoning
  • Weakness: small sample, not statistically generalizable
  • Focus groups add group dynamics (participants react to each other)
Key PointTheo's 12 one-on-one interviews revealed: "The tag makes me trust it's not stolen" — a motivation the survey never surfaced.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.6 — experiments and observations

Experiment
manipulating one variable while holding others constant to measure cause and effect.
Observation
watching and recording behavior without intervening.
  • Best for: what people actually do (vs. what they say they'll do)
  • Weakness: hard to control all variables in a real-world setting
Key PointTheo's observation: count how many shoppers pick up, inspect, or ask about tagged vs. untagged bikes on the sales floor.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.7 — A/B testing

A/B testing = comparing two versions (A and B) in a real-world setting to see which performs better on a defined metric.
  • One variable changes; everything else stays the same
  • Results are behavioral — based on what customers actually do
  • Common in: pricing, web design, email subject lines, product placement
Key PointA/B testing is the gold standard for "will they actually buy?" questions.
Lesson

Theo's A/B test

Setup:

  • 17 bikes total: 9 tagged at $95, 8 untagged at $75
  • Two consecutive Saturdays, same location, same display style

Results:

GroupSoldTotalSell rate
Tagged ($95)7978%
Untagged ($75)3838%
Key PointThe tag — not the price — appears to drive the sale. But one test is not proof. Theo needs more data.
Lesson

A/B math

Tagged: 7 / 9 = 77.8% ≈ 78%

Untagged: 3 / 8 = 37.5% ≈ 38%

Key PointA 40-percentage-point gap in a small sample. Suggestive, not conclusive — but strong enough to justify a follow-up.
Lesson

Follow-up: qualitative explains quantitative

The A/B test told Theo what happened (tagged bikes sold more). The interviews told him why.

12 one-on-one interviews with buyers who purchased tagged bikes:

ThemeFrequency
"The tag makes me trust it's not stolen"8 of 12
"I feel safer knowing someone checked the brakes"6 of 12
"It looks more professional"4 of 12
Exam NoteQuantitative data identifies the pattern. Qualitative data explains it. You need both.
Lesson

Keeping data honest

Research is only useful if the data is reliable. Two key safeguards:

1. Large, representative sample

  • Large enough to reduce the effect of random variation
  • Representative of the population you care about (not just who's convenient)

2. Neutral phrasing

  • Questions must not lead the respondent toward a particular answer
Lesson

Biased vs. neutral question phrasing

Biased (leading)Neutral
"Don't you think inspected bikes are safer?""How important is a documented inspection when buying a used bike?"
"Wouldn't you prefer a bike with a warranty tag?""Which features matter most when choosing a used bike: price, inspection tag, brand, or appearance?"
"Our bikes are the best value — agree?""How would you rate the value of this bike relative to its price?"
Key PointLeading questions produce useless data. The answer was baked into the question.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.8 — sample size and neutrality

Key PointThe two pillars of honest data:
PillarWhat it means
Sample size & representativenessEnough respondents, drawn from the population you're studying — not just your friends
Neutral phrasingQuestions that don't hint at the "right" answer
  • A biased question with 1,000 respondents gives you 1,000 useless answers
  • A neutral question with 15 carefully chosen respondents gives you 15 honest answers
Key PointQuality of the question matters more than quantity of responses.
Lesson

EK 2.3.C.9 — research does not guarantee prediction

Key PointResearch reduces uncertainty — it does not eliminate it.
  • A survey showing 72% would buy does not mean 72% will buy
  • An A/B test showing a 40-point gap in two Saturdays does not prove causation
  • Market conditions change, competitors react, customer moods shift
Key PointResearch improves the odds. It does not promise outcomes.
Lesson

The research confidence ladder

LevelWhat it gives you
No researchPure guess
Secondary onlyLandscape understanding — what's already known
Secondary + surveyQuantitative preferences from your target market
Secondary + survey + interviewsNumbers + motivations
Secondary + survey + interviews + A/B testNumbers + motivations + real-world behavioral data
Key PointEach layer adds confidence. None guarantees the future.

LO 2.3.D — Data Visualization

Turning numbers into patterns stakeholders can grasp.
Lesson

Why visualize data?

Exam NoteData visualizations turn raw data into patterns that stakeholders can grasp quickly.
  • A table of 60 numbers takes effort to read
  • A chart of those 60 numbers reveals a pattern in seconds
  • The right chart for the right data makes your case persuasive
Key PointThe goal is not decoration — it's communication.
Lesson

EK 2.3.D.1 — four chart types

Chart typeBest forVisual
Bar chartComparing values across categoriesBars side by side
Stacked bar chartShowing total + subcategories within each barBars divided into colored segments
Line chartShowing trend over timePoints connected by a line
Pie chartShowing part-to-whole (proportions of a total)Slices of a circle
Exam NotePick the chart that best shows your pattern. Don't pick a pie chart just because it looks nice.
Lesson

Bar chart — compare discrete categories

Use when: you want to compare a single value across categories.

Example: Average sale price of bikes by seller type (private, shop, certified reseller).

Seller typeAvg. price
Private$68
Shop$85
Certified reseller$98
Key PointA bar chart makes the price gap across seller types instantly visible.
Lesson

Stacked bar chart — total + parts

Use when: you want to show both the total and the composition within each category.

Example: Monthly bike sales by type (tagged vs. untagged).

MonthTaggedUntaggedTotal
January12820
February15520
March18422
Key PointThe bar height shows the total; the segments show what's inside.
Lesson

Line chart — trend over time

Use when: you want to show how a value changes over time.

Example: Average selling price of inspected bikes over 6 months.

Key PointIf the line slopes upward, the price is rising. If it dips, something changed. Time goes on the x-axis.
Lesson

Pie chart — part-to-whole

Use when: you want to show how parts make up a whole (proportions, percentages).

Example: What share of Theo's sales come from tagged vs. untagged bikes?

CategoryShare
Tagged70%
Untagged30%
Key PointLimitation: pie charts work best with few categories (3–5). More than that and the slices become hard to read.
Lesson

EK 2.3.D.2 — pick the right chart

Key PointThe question determines the chart.
If you want to show…Use…
Compare values across categoriesBar chart
Total + composition within each categoryStacked bar chart
Change over timeLine chart
Proportions of a wholePie chart
Exam NoteDon't default to the prettiest chart. Default to the one that makes your pattern obvious.
Lesson

EK 2.3.D.3 — bar charts in depth

Bar charts are the workhorse of data visualization on the AP exam.

  • Horizontal axis (x): categories (seller type, product, month)
  • Vertical axis (y): the measured value (price, quantity, percentage)
  • Each bar: one category's value
- Compare by height: taller bar = higher value
Exam NoteIf the exam asks "which chart best shows…" and the answer involves comparing categories, start with bar chart.
Lesson

Scatter plot — Theo's marketplace data

Scatter plot
plots individual data points on two axes to reveal relationships.

Theo's 60 marketplace listings: price vs. days listed

PatternWhat it means
Points cluster at low prices + low days listedCheap bikes sell fast
Points cluster at high prices + high days listedExpensive bikes sit unsold
Dead zone above $85Almost no bikes priced above $85 sold within 14 days
Key PointThe scatter plot reveals a price ceiling Theo didn't know existed. That's the power of visualization.
Lesson

Exam points to remember

- Market research = collect and analyze information to guide decisions
- Quantitative = numbers ("how many?"); Qualitative = words ("why?")
  • Desirable (want it?), Feasible (can we?), Viable (profitable?) — all three must pass
  • Research continues after launch — track, spot, adjust
- Secondary = existing data (cheap, fast); Primary = new data (specific, expensive)
- Hypothesis = testable assumption, written before collecting data
  • Match the method to the data type the hypothesis needs
- Honest data = representative sample + neutral phrasing
  • Research reduces uncertainty — it does not guarantee prediction (New Coke: 200,000 data points, $12M, still failed)
  • New Coke lesson: research measured the product (taste) but missed the brand (emotional attachment) — know what you're NOT measuring
  • Pick the chart that shows your pattern, not the prettiest one

Practice — 8 questions

Read → pause → commit to an answer → then reveal.
6 on one stimulus (Two Bucket Detail)· 2 standalone
Stimulus

Scenario

Tomas and Bree Herrera run Two Bucket Detail, a mobile car-detailing service. They offer two bundles:

BundleIncludesPrice
Inside OutInterior vacuum, wipe-down, exterior wash$65
ShowroomEverything in Inside Out + wax, tire shine, window polish$110

They want to know which bundle to promote more heavily. Tomas writes: "Customers who try Showroom once will prefer it over Inside Out on their next visit."

They run an A/B test over 4 weekends — alternating which bundle gets the banner on their booking page. They also conduct 10 follow-up interviews with customers who booked Showroom.

Practice Question
Q1What is Tomas's sentence?

Tomas's statement — "Customers who try Showroom once will prefer it over Inside Out on their next visit" — is best described as which?

AA market research finding
BA business hypothesis
CA customer segment
DA market observation
Answer B

Why

It is an assumption about customer behavior (prefer Showroom after trying it) that is stated so it can be tested (they can track repeat bookings by bundle). That is the CED's definition of a business hypothesis.

A — a "finding" comes after data collection. Tomas's sentence was written before** the test — it's a prediction, not a result.
Key PointA hypothesis = an assumption, testable, stated before the data.
Practice Question
Q2What is the 4-weekend comparison?

The 4-weekend test — alternating which bundle gets the banner — is best described as which?

AA focus group
BA market observation
CAn A/B test
DA customer survey
Answer C

Why

An A/B test compares two versions (A and B) in a real-world setting to see which performs better. Here, Version A = Inside Out banner, Version B = Showroom banner; the metric = which bundle customers book.

B — observation means watching without intervening.** Tomas and Bree are actively changing the banner — that's an experiment, not passive observation.
Key PointA/B test = change one variable, measure the difference.
Practice Question
Q3What are Bree's interviews?

Bree's 10 follow-up interviews with Showroom customers are best described as which?

AQuantitative data collected through a survey
BBehavioral data from an experiment
CQualitative data collected through follow-up questions
DA method that eliminates bias from the study
Answer C

Why

One-on-one interviews that ask customers to explain their experience produce qualitative data (words, not numbers). "Follow-up questions" is the key — the interviews dig into the why behind the A/B test results.

A — surveys collect structured, often quantitative data from many respondents. Interviews collect qualitative depth** from fewer people.
D — interviews are valuable but they do not eliminate bias.** A small sample and the interviewer's presence can both introduce bias.
Key PointInterviews explain the numbers. They don't sterilize them.
Practice Question
Q4Why research secondary first?

Before launching their A/B test and interviews, Tomas reviews local competitors' websites and pricing. Why is starting with secondary research a logical first step?

AIt guarantees the most accurate data for their specific question
BIt lets them understand the competitive landscape quickly and cheaply
CIt is the only way to collect qualitative data
DIt always provides data about their own customers
Answer B

Why

Secondary research is cost-effective — it uses data that already exists to help a business understand the landscape before investing in primary research. Tomas can see competitors' prices and bundles without spending a dollar.

A — secondary data may be outdated or not specific** to Tomas's exact question. It's a starting point, not a guarantee.
D — secondary data is about the market and competitors**, not necessarily about Tomas's own customers. That's what primary research is for.
Key PointSecondary first = cheap reconnaissance. Then fill the gaps with primary.
Practice Question
Q5Reading a survey table

Fernline Nursery surveys 200 customers about preferred plant types:

ResponseCount
Succulents120
Ferns50
Orchids30

A manager says: "30 out of our orchid-loving customers represent 15% of our respondents."

Is the manager's math correct?

AYes — 30 out of 200 = 15%
BNo — 30 out of 120 = 25%
CNo — 30 out of 50 = 60%
DYes — but only if orchid buyers are the majority
Answer A

Why

The base is the total number of respondents: 200. 30 / 200 = 0.15 = 15%. The manager's math is correct.

The trap in the trap: B calculates 30/120 = 25%, which uses the wrong base (succulent lovers, not total respondents). When the exam gives you a percentage, always check what the base is.

Key PointAlways check the BASE. A percentage is only as honest as the denominator.
Practice Question
Q6Which chart for the pottery studio?

A pottery studio tracks monthly revenue from three sources: classes, studio rentals, and retail sales. The owner wants to show the total monthly revenue and how much each source contributes. Which chart type is best?

ALine chart
BPie chart
CStacked bar chart
DBar chart (grouped)
Answer C

Why

A stacked bar chart shows both the total (bar height) and the composition (colored segments within each bar) — exactly what the owner needs: total revenue per month + how much each source contributes.

A — a line chart shows trend over time** for a single variable. It doesn't show composition within each month.
D — a grouped bar chart compares categories side by side, but makes it harder to see the total** at a glance.
Key PointStacked bar = total + parts. That's the exact match.
Practice Question
Q7Interpret the scatter plot
Theo plots 60 marketplace listings: x-axis = price, y-axis = days listed. Most points above $85 cluster at 14+ days listed. Most points below $75 cluster at under 5 days. What does this pattern suggest?
AHigher-priced bikes always sell faster
BThere may be a price ceiling above which bikes sit unsold
CPrice has no effect on how quickly bikes sell
DAll bikes sell within two weeks regardless of price
Answer B

Why

The data shows that bikes priced above $85 tend to sit for 14+ days, while bikes below $75 sell in under 5 days. This suggests a price ceiling — a point above which demand drops sharply.

A — says the opposite of what the data shows. Higher prices are associated with slower** sales, not faster.
Key PointRead the axes. Read the cluster. Don't assume the direction.
Practice Question
Q8Research reduces uncertainty

Which best explains why market research does not guarantee a successful business decision?

AResearch is too expensive to be worthwhile
BCustomer preferences, market conditions, and competitor actions can change after the research is conducted
COnly large corporations can conduct reliable research
DQuantitative data is always more reliable than qualitative data
Answer B

Why

Research captures a snapshot of the market at a point in time. Conditions shift — customers change preferences, competitors react, external factors (PESTEL) evolve. Research reduces uncertainty but cannot predict the future with certainty.

A — research has costs, but secondary research is cheap** and even small-scale primary research can be affordable. Cost does not mean research is worthless.
Key PointResearch improves the odds. It does not promise outcomes.
