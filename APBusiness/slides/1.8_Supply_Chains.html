Opening question

You know who owns the business (1.7), who does what, and how the departments work.

Key PointBut where does the product actually come from — and how does it get to the customer?

Sunday-night inventory count. The candy seller flips a chocolate bar over and suddenly realizes he has no idea where it comes from. Cocoa grew on a farm in another hemisphere. A factory melted, molded, and wrapped it. A warehouse held it. A truck moved it.

Only then did his backpack enter the story.

Lesson

Artisan or mass production — definition

Before anything moves through a chain, somebody has to make it. Two poles:

🤲 Hand & skill

Artisan

Relies on skilled labor + close attention to detail.

Output: smaller quantities.

🏭 Machine & scale

Mass production

Relies on technology, assembly lines, machinery.

Output: far larger quantities.

Key PointQuality can come from either pole. Artisan does not mean better; mass-produced does not mean worse.
Lesson

ArtisanMass production
At the parkThe cookie parent — hours of skilled piping per batch, two dozen cookies, premium priceThe chocolate bars — machines poured thousands of identical bars in the same time
What drives itHand skill, attention to detail, small batchesTechnology, assembly lines, sheer volume
Key PointThe cookie parent and the chocolate bar sit at opposite poles of the same question: how do you make the product?
Lesson

Three inputs determine the choice — definition

Which pole a business chooses depends on three inputs:

  1. Customer priorities (first) — buyers who prize customization lean toward artisan; buyers who prize low price lean toward mass production
  2. Core competencies (second, from 1.5) — a business built on handcraft skill should not suddenly pretend to be a factory
  3. Competitive landscape (third, from 1.2) — entering a market of cheap mass-produced rivals with a mid-priced product satisfies nobody
Lesson

Three inputs determine the choice — in the examples

The cookie parent read all three correctly:

InputHer read
Customer prioritiesParents at the park pay extra for birthday-worthy cookies — customization wins
Core competencies (1.5)Her skill is decoration, not factory logistics — stay artisan
Competitive landscape (1.2)No rival at the park can copy her piping — she owns the niche
Key PointAll three pointed the same direction. That's why artisan was the right call — not because artisan is "better," but because it matched her customers, her skills, and her market.

Five supplier factors — definition

Every link in a chain is a choice. Businesses weigh five factors when choosing suppliers:

FactorVisible?
CostOn the price sheet
QualityOn the price sheet / specs
EfficiencyOn the delivery schedule
ConvenienceOn the delivery schedule
RiskHides
Key PointRisk is the factor that hides. Natural disasters, political instability, resource shortages, production errors, and supplier reputation can all delay delivery or raise costs.

He splits the order: the wholesaler carries the bulk, and a supermarket run on Friday night backstops the risk.

Lesson

Supplier decisions — the exam habit

Key PointEvery one of the five factors, plus a backup plan, belongs in a supplier answer.

The exam loves to offer a cheap option with hidden risk and ask you to choose. The strong answer:

  1. Names all five factors — not just cost
  2. Prices in the risk — the factor that hides
  3. Mentions a backup plan
Key PointBuying inputs is not outsourcing. Every producer buys raw materials. The question is always which supplier, why, and what happens if they fail.

Lesson

Scaling — definition

Some cost-focused businesses go further and scale: build higher-capacity chains so that revenue increases outrun cost increases.

The mechanism: the second batch reuses the same oven, permits, and order system — the fixed costs get spread across twice the units while every unit still sells at full price.

Before scalingAfter scaling
Units20,00040,000
Revenue$80,000$160,000 (+100%)
Total costs$60,000$96,000 (+60%)
Profit$20,000$64,000
Key PointRevenue grew 100%; costs grew only 60%. That gap is the entire point of scaling.
Lesson

Scaling — in the examples

If the snack box doubled batches for the same Saturday:

What changesWhat stays fixed
Inventory cost doublesThe oven (same one, second batch)
Prep time roughly doublesThe permits (already paid)
The order system (same group chat)
Key PointFixed costs (oven, permits, order system) spread across twice the units. Each unit still sells at full price — so revenue grows faster than costs.
Exam NoteThat is the scaling mechanism the exam tests. Know it as a formula: fixed costs spread, revenue outpaces costs, profit accelerates.

Lesson

Ford (1913) — mass production and scaling

Ford's assembly line turned artisan car-making into mass production.

  • Before: one skilled team built an entire car — 12 hours per vehicle
  • After: each worker performed one task on a moving line — 93 minutes per car
  • Price dropped from $850 to $260
Key PointCustomer priority shifted from customization (artisan) to low price (mass production). The chain was built entirely around cost-cutting through scale.

AP connection: the three inputs (1.8.A) — Ford read customer priorities, matched its competencies to factory production, and exploited a landscape where no rival could match the price.

Lesson

Toyota (1950s) — just-in-time

Toyota's JIT system synchronized suppliers so precisely that a late part shut down the whole factory.

  • Not bigger — tighter
  • Supplier selection prioritized reliability over cost
  • The factory held almost no inventory — parts arrived exactly when needed
Key PointWhich of the five supplier factors did Toyota prioritize? Efficiency and reliability — and when that factor failed, the entire line stopped.

AP connection: risk (the factor that hides) plus the need for a backup plan. A single late shipment could halt production for a day.

Lesson

Walmart (1962) — distribution centers

Walmart turned warehouses into distribution centers.

  • A warehouse stores. A distribution center stores and forwards.
  • This one change cut delivery from days to hours
  • Created competitive advantage through faster delivery at lower cost
Key PointThe warehouse-to-distribution-center distinction is not just vocabulary — it is a strategic choice that reshapes the entire chain.

AP connection: two facilities (1.8.B) — the exam tests whether you can tell them apart. Walmart's advantage came from choosing the right one.

Lesson

Globalization (1990s) — chains stretch

Supply chains went global — raw materials from one country, manufacturing in another, assembly in a third, sale in a fourth.

  • Cost advantage: labor and materials cheaper overseas
  • New risk: one disruption halfway around the world could stop your entire chain
  • The longer the chain, the more links can break
Key PointThe risk was hiding in the length of the chain itself.

AP connection: global supply chains are longer and more complex — each stage adds a link that can fail. The five supplier factors apply globally, with risk amplified by distance.

Lesson

Amazon (2000s) — the service supply chain

Amazon turned distribution into a service — and then sold the service itself.

  • Fulfillment centers, delivery fleets, same-day logistics
  • AWS: the infrastructure became the product
  • The supply chain was no longer about goods — it was about people, resources, and a delivery system
Key PointThe service supply chain — tutors, lawn-care crews, and now Amazon — runs on the same three parts: people, resources, delivery system.

AP connection: service supply chains (1.8.B) — when the scenario describes hiring, equipping, and delivering with no factory, name the three parts.

Lesson

COVID (2020s) — risk materializes

COVID-19 exposed every hidden risk at once:

- Single-source suppliers in one country = no backup
  • Chip shortages halted car production worldwide
  • Shipping containers sat in the wrong ports
  • The risk had been hiding for decades
Key PointEvery supplier decision should include a backup plan. The factor that hides eventually shows up — and when it does, chains without a backup stop.

AP connection: risk is the factor that hides (1.8.C). COVID was the exam question nobody wanted: "What happens when the risk actually materializes?"

Case study — Zara's supply chain

Lesson

Zara — the strategy: speed

Zara's competitive advantage is speed — getting new designs to stores faster than any rival.

  • 2 weeks from design to store shelf (industry: 6 months)
  • Small batches — if a style doesn't sell, it's gone in weeks
  • Frequent refresh — stores receive new inventory twice a week
Exam NoteThe strategy decided the chain. Every link — design, manufacturing, distribution, retail — was built to serve one goal: faster than everyone else.
Lesson

Zara — the chain: every stage built for speed

StageWhat Zara does differentlyAP 1.8 concept
DesignIn-house design team in Spain — no outsourcingCore competency (1.5) kept in-house
Raw materialsFabric purchased in advance (undyed) — color decided laterPre-positioned materials cut lead time
ManufacturingOwn factories in Spain + nearby countries — not outsourced to AsiaSupplier control for speed, not cost
DistributionOne central distribution center in Spain — all products flow through itWarehouse vs. distribution center (1.8.B)
Retail6,000+ stores worldwide, inventory refreshed twice a weekShort chain = less risk
Lesson

Zara — the distribution center is the advantage

Zara built one massive central distribution center in Arteixo, Spain.

  • Every garment — whether destined for Tokyo, New York, or São Paulo — passes through this one facility
  • A warehouse stores. Zara's center stores and forwards — garments arrive and ship within hours
  • The entire chain is designed so that a design sketch on Monday can be on a store shelf by Friday
Key PointThis is the warehouse vs. distribution center distinction made real. Zara doesn't store inventory — it moves inventory. The center is a relay, not a warehouse.
Lesson

Zara — supplier selection: speed over cost

Zara's supplier choices break the "cheapest wins" rule:

FactorZara's choiceWhy
CostHigher — own factories in Spain cost more than outsourcing to AsiaAccepted as the price of speed
QualityHigh — in-house control over every stepReputation depends on it
EfficiencyMaximum — 2-week turnaround is the #1 priorityThe whole chain exists for this
ConvenienceCentralized — one distribution center for the worldSimplifies coordination
RiskLower — short chain, fewer borders, fewer linksLess distance = fewer things can break
Exam NoteZara traded cost for speed. The five supplier factors don't all point the same direction — a business must choose which factor to prioritize based on its strategy.
Lesson

Zara — barriers to entry

Zara's supply chain is nearly impossible to copy:

  • Own factories — competitors outsource and lose control
  • One central distribution center — requires massive investment
  • 2-week turnaround — built over decades, not overnight
  • Small-batch, high-refresh model — requires a completely different chain than traditional fashion
Key PointThe supply chain itself is the barrier to entry. Rivals can copy a dress design in days — but they can't copy the chain that delivers it in 2 weeks.

AP connection: this is the exclusivity-tuned chain strategy (EK 1.8.C.3) — not through a legal agreement, but through a chain so specialized that competitors can't replicate it.

Stimulus

Scenario

Nadia Farouk owns Copper Kettle Candles, pouring small batches by hand with premium wax and charging approximately twice what machine-made candles cost. Customers praise the quality and custom scents.

Nadia buys wax from an overseas supplier at a price 20% below local — but 2 of the last 6 shipments arrived weeks late, and one contained the wrong blend.

A boutique chain offers a deal: feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores if Nadia signs an exclusive retail rights agreement.

> Watch for three decision points: production approach, supplier choice, and the exclusive agreement.

Practice Question
Q1Classify production approach

Nadia's method of pouring small batches by hand with premium wax is best described as which?

AMass production, because she sells in multiple locations
BArtisan production, because she relies on skilled labor and attention to detail in smaller quantities
COutsourcing, because she buys wax from an overseas supplier
DA service supply chain, because she provides custom scents

Pause. Ask: does she use machinery and assembly lines to produce large quantities, or skilled hands and careful attention in small batches?

-->

Answer B

Why

Artisan production relies on skilled labor and close attention to detail, producing smaller quantities. Nadia pours by hand, uses premium materials, and makes small batches — every marker points to artisan.

C — buying inputs is not outsourcing.** Every producer buys raw materials. Nadia makes the candles herself; she buys wax the same way a baker buys flour.
A — selling in multiple locations does not change the production method.** She could sell in 100 stores and still be artisan if she pours every candle by hand.
Key PointClassify by how the product is made, not by how many places it is sold.
Practice Question
Q2Supplier decision

Nadia's overseas wax supplier offers a price 20% below local, but 2 of 6 recent shipments arrived late and one had the wrong blend. Which analysis best supports a sound supplier decision?

AChoose the overseas supplier because lower cost always increases profit
BChoose the local supplier because reliability is the only factor that matters
CWeigh the lower cost against the risks — including late deliveries, wrong shipments, and potential damage to reputation — and consider a backup plan
DChoose the overseas supplier because customers will never know about delivery problems
Answer C

Why

The five supplier factors are cost, quality, efficiency, convenience, and risk. The overseas supplier wins on cost but loses on reliability and risk (late deliveries, wrong blend). A sound decision weighs all five factors and includes a backup plan.

A — "always" is an absolute.** Lower cost does not always increase profit if late deliveries cause stockouts and lost customers.
B — reliability is one factor, not the only** factor. The question says "only" — that is as much of a trap as "always."
Key PointWeigh all five factors, with risk priced in. Name the backup plan.
Practice Question
Q3Exclusive retail agreement

The boutique chain offers to feature Copper Kettle in 12 stores if Nadia signs an exclusive retail rights agreement. This agreement would most directly create competitive advantage through which mechanism?

ABarriers to entry — the exclusive agreement locks up retail shelf space, preventing competitors from accessing those stores
BCost leadership — selling in 12 stores reduces per-unit production costs
CEconomies of scale — more stores means Nadia can switch to mass production
DGuaranteed long-term profits from the partnership
Answer A

Why

An exclusive retail rights agreement is a barrier to entry — it locks up shelf space so competitors cannot access those 12 stores. That is one of the three strategy-to-chain mechanisms the exam tests.

D — "guaranteed" is an absolute.** The deal shifts the odds in Nadia's favor; it guarantees nothing. The boutique chain could drop her line, close stores, or renegotiate.
C — selling in 12 stores does not automatically mean Nadia should switch to mass production. She is an artisan producer; the agreement gives her shelf space**, not a factory.
Key PointExclusive agreements create barriers to entry. They do not guarantee profits.
Practice Question
Q4Order the supply chain

Which best identifies the order of the supply chain for a mass-produced good, from start to finish?

AManufacturing, raw materials, retail store, warehouse, customer
BRaw materials, warehouse, manufacturing, customer, distribution center
CDistribution center, raw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, customer
DRaw materials, manufacturing, warehouse, distribution center or retail store, customer
Answer D

The chain runs in one direction:

Key PointRaw materials to manufacturing to warehouse to distribution/retail to customer
B fails twice — it puts the warehouse before manufacturing (the warehouse stores finished goods), and it ends with a distribution center after the customer.**
Key PointWarehouse comes after manufacturing and before distribution. Never after the customer.
Practice Question
Q5Scaling table

BatchBox Snacks doubled its production scale last year:

Before scalingAfter scaling
Units20,00040,000
Revenue$80,000$160,000
Total costs$60,000$96,000
Profit$20,000$64,000

Which conclusion is best supported by the table?

AScaling failed, because total costs increased by $36,000
BScaling succeeded, because revenue grew 100% while costs grew only 60%, raising profit from $20,000 to $64,000
CScaling guarantees higher profits every year going forward
DThe business should return to its original scale
Answer B

Compute both growth rates:

- Revenue: $80,000 to $160,000 = +100%
- Costs: $60,000 to $96,000 = +60%
  • Profit: $20,000 to $64,000 confirms it

Revenue growth outran cost growth. That is the scaling mechanism.

A reads one number and stops — costs rose (as they do when production doubles); the question is whether revenue rose faster.**
C converts one good year into a permanent promise — the "guarantees" alarm** rings here as everywhere.
Key PointRead both growth rates. Compare them. One number alone is never enough.
Practice Question
Q6Service supply chain

An online tutoring company hires tutors, develops lesson materials, and delivers sessions through a video platform. Which best describes this company's supply chain?

ARaw materials flowing to a manufacturing facility for assembly
BA warehouse storing finished lessons until customers order them
CAcquiring tutors, learning resources, and a virtual delivery platform to provide the service
DServices cannot have supply chains
Answer C

A service supply chain acquires the employees, the resources, and the delivery systems needed to provide the service — tutors, materials, and a video platform are those three parts.

B borrows the goods chain — warehouses store physical products; a tutoring session is delivered live.**
D is the misconception this topic exists to correct — services absolutely run supply chains**, built from people, resources, and delivery systems instead of trucks and pallets.
Key PointWhen the scenario names hiring, equipping, and delivering — with no factory — it is a service supply chain. The answer names three parts.
