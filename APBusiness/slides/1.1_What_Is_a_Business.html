Our Running Example

Meet the candy seller

  • Bought in bulk over the weekend; sells bars for about 3× what he paid
  • By Friday the bag is empty → he restocks
The claimHe meets the course's complete definition of a business — and we'll prove it today.

Our Running Example

The candy seller — the operation

Every concept today — and across all of Unit 1 — maps onto one business.

Source

Buys a 20-bar box at the supermarket on weekends (bulk price).

Sell

Between classes, in the hallway, one bar at a time.

Price

About 3× what he paid (≈ $1.50 a bar).

Restock

By Friday the bag is empty → the weekend run repeats.

Our Running Example

Why he has customers — and why he's a real business

Why he has customers

Students want a snack mid-day, the vending machines are empty or off-limits, and leaving campus is against the rules → he solves a real problem at the moment it hits.

Why he's a full business

He makes the decisions, keeps the profit, and carries the risk — we'll even name his legal form in 1.7.

Core Definition

What is a business?

Definition
A business is an organization or entity that produces and distributes products.

products = goods, services, or both

What the definition leaves outsize · buildings · employees · legal paperwork
Think firstA business can be any size, face-to-face or virtual. By this definition — are Bombas, 凡客, and the candy seller all businesses?
Applying It

What is a business? — in the examples

Produces?Distributes?Business?

凡客 (VANCL)apparel (goods)online store✓

TakeawayProducing / distributing products is what counts — not size, buildings, or paperwork.

Key Distinction

Goods vs. services

Goods

Tangible items — a candy bar, a phone, a desk.

Services

Work performed for compensation — a haircut, a tutoring session, a flight.

How to classifyClassify by what the customer is primarily paying for in the scenario.
Think firstDoes Bombas sell goods, services, or both? What about 凡客?
Applying It

Goods vs. services — in the examples

  • Bombas: socks = goods; customer support + easy returns = a service → sells both
  • 凡客 (VANCL): the apparel = goods (its site + customer service = a service)
  • Candy seller: the bar = a good (the convenience of hallway availability is the service wrapped around it)
Two Money Terms

Revenue & Profit

Revenue

Money earned from selling, before expenses.

Profit

What remains after all expenses are subtracted.

Profit = Revenue − Total Cost
Think firstHow do 凡客 and Bombas each turn revenue into profit?
Applying It

Revenue & Profit — two models, same formula

  • 凡客 (VANCL): low price × high volume (fast fashion) — until over-expansion broke the model
  • Bombas: premium price × fewer units (makes 2 pairs per sale — one sold, one donated)
  • Candy seller: revenue = everything collected this week; profit = what's left after the supermarket run + restocking
Key Distinction

Customer vs. consumer

Customer

The individual or business that purchases.

Consumer

The individual who uses it (whether or not they bought it).

Both in oneBuy your own lunch → you're both in one transaction.
Think firstWhere is the buyer NOT the user? (Hint: a donated pair, or the candy seller's $3/two-bar sale.)
Applying It

Customer vs. consumer — in the examples

  • Bombas buy-one-give-one: the buyer = customer; the donated pair is used by a shelter guest who never bought it = a different consumer
  • Candy seller: see the $3 / two-bar split (next slide) ↓
The rulePurchase defines the customer; use defines the consumer.
Example

Core Concept

Needs, wants, market opportunities

Need

Essential for survival (food, water, shelter, basic healthcare).

Want

Improves life, not essential.

Market opportunity

A customer problem / need / want a business might address → every business starts at one of these.

Think firstIs a pair of socks a need or a want? What about a flavored drink?
Applying It

Needs, wants, opportunities — in the examples

  • Socks are a need (basic clothing) — but a $12 seamless pair is a want
  • Clothing is a need — 凡客's fast fashion is a want
  • Candy seller's opportunity: students want a snack mid-day → he addresses it
  • Xiaohongshu (小红书): info gap on overseas shopping (2013) — a want tied to its era (outbound-travel boom + fear of fakes) → a market opportunity that barely existed a decade earlier
DefinitionA market opportunity = a need/want a business might address. Every business starts at one.
Core Concept

Problem-solution fit

Definition
The match between a customer problem and a product built to solve it.
Think firstWhat problem does Bombas solve? 凡客? The candy seller?
Applying It

Problem-solution fit — in the examples

Bombas

Problem = socks that hurt + shelters short on socks → a better sock and a donated pair.

凡客 (VANCL)

Problem = affordable, decent-quality fashion → "人民快时尚".

Candy seller

Snack want + empty/off-limits vending machines → candy in the hallway the moment it hits.

Uber / DiDi

Unpredictable taxis + unclear pricing → match riders to drivers with an upfront price.

Xiaohongshu (小红书)Problem = "which overseas products are worth buying — and can I trust the seller?" → solution = real-user reviews (a trust/information fix, not a product fix).
Core Concept

"You can't serve everyone"

A business cannot satisfy all potential customers → it must select.

Selection questions

Severity? · How many share it? · Can we solve it better? · Will they pay more than it costs? · Can we reach them?

Think firstWhich customers did Bombas choose? What did 凡客 do instead?
WarningAn answer choice that says "serve everyone" = the wrong answer.
Applying It

Selection — won vs. lost

Won by selecting

  • Bombas chose premium, mission-minded buyers — not the cheapest-sock market
  • Candy seller works only his own grade's hallway — a deliberate, narrow selection

Lost by trying to serve everyone

  • 凡客 (VANCL) — started with shirts, then sold everything; inventory blew up and the brand thinned out → collapsed
Core Concept

Value

Definition
The worth or benefit of a product to customers = the highest price they'd willingly pay.
RelationshipValue rises as problem-solution fit improves.
Think firstHow much would a Bombas buyer willingly pay? A 凡客 buyer?
Applying It

Value — in the examples

  • Bombas buyers willingly pay ~$12 (comfort + mission = high perceived value)
  • 凡客 (VANCL) targeted price-sensitive buyers → value delivered via affordability
  • Candy seller: the same bar is worth more during 3rd period than next to a supermarket checkout
The Topic's Core Pair

Value creation vs. value capture

Value creationValue capture
Is aboutthe customerthe business
Meansprovides a product that responds to a problem/need/wantcharges a price higher than cost
Flowsbenefit to the customerprofit to the business
Order mattersBoth required, in order: create first, then capture.
Think firstHow does Bombas create AND capture? How did 凡客?
Applying It

Creation vs. capture — in the examples

  • Bombas: creates value (comfort + mission) and captures it (premium price)
  • 凡客 (VANCL): created value (affordable fashion) and captured it — until over-expansion eroded both
  • Candy seller: creates (snack at the moment of want) and captures ($1.50 − $0.50 = $1.00/bar)
  • MoviePass: created value but captured none → shut down (coming up)
Watch the trapThe most common exam trap = putting the capture definition inside a creation question (we'll see it in Q3).
Worked Example

The candy numbers

He buys a 20-bar box for $10 → cost = $0.50 / bar. Sells each for $1.50.

Value captured = Price − Cost = $1.50 − $0.50 = $1.00 / bar

Customer side

A student who'd have paid $2 pays $1.50 → keeps $0.50 of benefit.

Why the trade happens

Both sides end up better off → that's why the trade happens voluntarily.

*Bombas's capture = price − the cost of making 2 pairs — one sold, one donated.

Cautionary Tale

When you create but don't capture — MoviePass

  • ~$10 / month to see a movie in theaters every day
  • MoviePass paid theaters near full price for the tickets members used
  • A member watching 4 movies/month cost the company several × $10
  • Price − cost was negative → shut down 2019
Value creation (people loved it) ✓ Value capture (price > cost) ✗
Cautionary Tale

When you create but don't capture — ofo

ofo 小黄车 (China) — same failure, different country:

  • Bikes on every corner, dirt cheap; users loved it
  • But each bike's wear + upkeep cost more than the revenue → burned through billions, collapsed 2018–19
WarningPopularity + price below cost = value creation WITHOUT value capture.
Core Concept

Value proposition

Definition
A clear statement of how a product solves a problem and why choose it over alternatives.
For [target customer] who has [problem], our product delivers [benefit]. Unlike [the alternative]…
Think firstFill in the template for Bombas. Then for the candy seller.
Applying It

Value proposition — in the examples

Bombas

"For people who want comfortable socks, a premium seamless pair — and for every pair you buy, we donate a pair to someone in need."

Candy seller

"For students stuck on campus who want a snack now, a candy bar in the hallway for $1.50. Unlike an empty vending machine or a store they can't go to."

RecipeOne sentence naming the customer, the problem, the solution, and the edge.
Review

Exam points to remember

Business = what it does, not its size

Classify goods/services by what's primarily paid for

Customer vs consumer → underline paid vs used

Value capture = Price − Cost

"Serve everyone" = wrong answer

Popularity + price below cost = creation without capture

Check Yourself

Practice — 6 questions

3 on one stimulus (LumiDesk) 3 standalone
Read → pause → commit to an answer → then reveal.
Stimulus

LumiDesk

Scenario

LumiDesk makes height-adjustable study desks for students in small apartments.

  • Cost to manufacture: $90
  • Customers value it at: $260 (sits ↔ stands, folds flat)
  • Sells for: $180
  • Tutoring centers now buy desks → students use them in lessons
C = $90 value = $260 P = $180
Keep these three numbersThey power all three LumiDesk questions.
Q1 Who is the consumer?

For desks bought by tutoring centers, which best identifies the consumer?

Athe tutoring centers
BLumiDesk
Cthe students who use the desks during lessons
Dthe parents who pay tuition to the tutoring centers
PausePoint at "who paid" and "who used" in the scenario before answering.
Answer C

Why

The consumer is the one who uses it. The centers bought (customer); the students use them (consumers).

Traps: A = the customer placed in a consumer question. D = a real but different transaction (parents paying tuition).
RuleAnswer the transaction the question points at.
Q2 Compute value captured

What is the value captured by LumiDesk per desk at the current price?

A$80
B$90
C$170
D$260
Answer B ($90)
Value captured = Price − Cost = $180 − $90 = $90
Trap: A ($80) = $260 − $180 (value − price) → that's the customer's kept benefit, built from the same three numbers — which is exactly why it's there.
StrategyIn a capture question, write price − cost before you look at the choices.
Q3 Why is it value creation?

Which best explains why LumiDesk demonstrates value creation?

Asold at a price higher than manufacturing cost
Bresponds to the space problem faced by students in small apartments
Cgenerates revenue from individuals and tutoring centers
Dmanufacturing cost is low relative to competitors
Answer B

Why

Value creation = responds to a customer's problem / need / want. The small-apartment space problem is what the product was built for.

Trap: A = the value-capture definition placed inside a creation question — the most common trap in this topic.
RuleCreation → the customer's problem; capture → price & cost.
Q4 Problem-solution fit

A snack company sees gym members want high-protein food right after workouts, and develops a refrigerated protein snack in gym vending machines. Which concept does this best illustrate?

Avalue capture
Bbarrier to entry
Cproblem-solution fit
Da need converted into a want
Answer C

Why

A want was identified, a matching product built, placed at the point the want occurs.

Trap: B — a real Topic 1.2 term used too early. Recognizing a word isn't enough — it must match the scenario.
Q5 Goods vs. services

HomeGuard: a one-time price for a camera + doorbell plus a monthly fee for 24-hour monitoring through an app. Which best describes its products?

Agoods only
Bservices only
Cboth goods and services
Dneither
Answer C

Why

Camera = good; monitoring = service.

Trap: D mixes two vocabulary sets. Needs/wants = why buy; goods/services = what's sold.
Q6 Choosing customers

A new meal-delivery startup has limited funding. Which best reflects how businesses address market opportunities?

Aserve every possible customer in the city
Bselect a specific group whose problem you can solve better than competitors
Ctarget the customers with the mildest problems
Ddelay choosing customers until the product is perfected
Answer B

Why

A business cannot satisfy all customers → it selects.

Trap: A — "serve everyone" reads as ambition but is the wrong answer (and will appear on the real exam).
