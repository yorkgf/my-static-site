LO 2.4.A — Six stages from idea to market

Part 1: Product Development

Lesson

What is product development?

customer problem → a product people pay for

not one moment — six stages, each with its own key question

Key PointThe goal at every stage is the same: reduce uncertainty before spending more money.
Lesson

The six stages at a glance

The goal at every stage: reduce uncertainty before spending more money.

1

Generate Idea

What customer problem can we solve?

2

Screen & Refine

Is this idea feasible?

3

Develop Prototype

What will it look and function like?

4

Test Concept

Does it solve a real problem for real people?

5

Develop MVP

What is the simplest version customers will pay for?

6

Refine with Feedback

What do early users want changed?

Lesson

Stage 1 — Generate Idea

Ideas come from many sources:

  • Customer input — complaints, reviews, surveys, social media
  • Brainstorming — team sessions, design thinking workshops
  • Observation — watching how people actually use (or struggle with) existing products
  • Trend analysis — spotting gaps in the market
Key PointBest ideas start with a problem, not a product.

“I want to build X” → you may build nobody needs

If you start with "People struggle with Y," you already have a customer.

Lesson

Stage 2 — Screen & Refine

filter out weak concepts before spending money

Screening criteria:

FactorQuestion to Ask
Market sizeAre enough people experiencing this problem?
CompetitionIs someone already solving it well? If so, can we do it differently?
CostCan we build this at a price customers will accept?
RegulatoryAre there legal barriers (safety, patents, licenses)?
Key PointScreening is an internal exercise — you haven't talked to customers yet.
Lesson

Stage 3 — Develop Prototype

A prototype is a preliminary model of the product.

It can range from:

  • A sketch or 3D render (visual prototype)
  • A cardboard mockup (physical prototype)
  • A clickable app wireframe (digital prototype)
  • A working model with real materials (functional prototype)
Key Pointprototypes learn, not sell — each version teaches
Lesson

Stage 4 — Test Concept with Customers

Concept testing means presenting the idea to real potential customers to check:

  • Does this solve a problem they actually have?
  • Would they pay for it?
  • What would make it better?

This is different from idea screening:

- Screening = internal evaluation (costs, competition, regulations)
- Concept testing = external validation (what do real customers think?)
Key PointA product that excites your team but bores your customers is not a product.
Lesson

Stage 5 — Develop MVP

MVP = Minimum Viable Product

most basic version = core features only, priced to buy

MVP is NOT...MVP IS...
A prototype (built to explore)A product (built to sell)
Full-featuredCore features only
PerfectGood enough to learn from
FreeSomething customers pay for
Key PointMVP answers: “will real people pay real money?”
Lesson

Stage 6 — Refine Based on Feedback

After early customers use the MVP, collect feedback and improve:

  • Which features do they love? → Keep and strengthen
  • Which features do they ignore? → Consider removing
  • What is missing? → Add in next version
  • What frustrates them? → Fix immediately

iterate: v1 → feedback → v2 → feedback → v3…

Key Pointnever one-shot — products evolve through feedback loops
Lesson

  • Surveyed 50 students about meal planning habits
  • Built a version with only 3 recipes and a shopping list

Which stage are they in?

Lesson

Your turn — Stage check answer

They are in Stage 6 — Refine Based on Feedback.

  • Wireframes → Stage 3 (prototype) ✅ done
  • Surveyed students → Stage 4 (concept testing) ✅ done
  • Built a basic version → Stage 5 (MVP) ✅ done

Lesson

🔑 Exam points to remember — Product Development

#Point
1The six stages are sequential — each reduces uncertainty before the next investment
2Screening (internal) ≠ Concept testing (external/customer-facing)

Dyson — A product development story

Dyson previously built the Ballbarrow (ball-wheel barrow) — sold well

renovating → noticed the vacuum lost suction when the bag filled

sawmill → a 30-ft cyclone separates dust with no filter

Key PointDyson's idea: What if a vacuum could use cyclone technology instead of a bag?
Lesson

Dyson — 5,127 prototypes

Dyson spent five years (1979–1984) building prototypes in a barn behind his house.

  • Prototype 1: Did not work at all
  • Prototype 500: Partial suction, but air leaked
  • Prototype 2,000: Cyclone worked, but the machine was too large
  • Prototype 5,127: Finally worked perfectly

no income — wife taught art to support the family

Key Pointa failed prototype is a lesson for the next
Lesson

Dyson — Rejection and MVP

When Dyson approached vacuum manufacturers with his working prototype:

  • Every major brand refused to license it
  • they sold billions of bags — a bagless vacuum kills that stream

So Dyson manufactured it himself:

  • Launched the G-Force in Japan (1986) — sold for $2,000, a premium price
- Japan launch = MVP test — would customers pay for bagless technology?
  • Japanese customers loved it → validated the concept
Key PointG-Force = the MVP — simplest version, sold to test demand
Lesson

Dyson — From MVP to global brand

After Japan validated demand, Dyson launched the DC01 in the UK (1993):

  • Priced twice as high as competitor vacuums
  • Became the #1 selling vacuum in the UK within 18 months
  • Expanded to the US, Europe, and Asia

Key product development stages in Dyson's story:

StageDyson's Action
Generate IdeaSaw industrial cyclone at sawmill
Screen & RefineAnalyzed vacuum market, found bag pain point
Develop Prototype5,127 versions over 5 years
Test ConceptShowed working prototype to manufacturers (rejected)
Develop MVPG-Force launch in Japan (1986)
Refine with FeedbackDC01 in UK (1993) — refined design, price, and features

LO 2.4.B — Building identity and trust

Part 2: Branding

Lesson

What is a brand?

name · design · symbol → identifies and differentiates from competitors

Examples:

  • Apple — minimalist design, innovation, premium pricing
  • Nike — athletic performance, "Just Do It" attitude
  • Dyson — engineering excellence, premium innovation
Key Pointbrand ≠ logo — a brand is what customers feel seeing the logo
Lesson

Brand vs. Branding vs. Brand Equity

ConceptWhat It IsExample
BrandThe identifiable mark (name, logo, symbol)The Dyson logo and name
BrandingThe strategic process of shaping customer perception over timeDyson's consistent message: "Better engineering, no compromise"
Brand EquityThe added financial value from customer recognition, trust, and loyaltyCustomers pay $500+ for a Dyson vacuum when a $100 vacuum exists
Key PointBranding builds brand equity over time. You cannot buy equity — you earn it.
Lesson

Why does branding matter?

Branding creates three advantages:

  1. Differentiation — In a crowded market, a strong brand stands out
  2. Trust and loyalty — Customers return to brands they recognize and trust
  3. Pricing power — Strong brands can charge more because customers perceive higher value
Key Pointbrand equity: $50 logo tee outsells a $10 plain one
Lesson

Building brand equity — Consistency is key

Brand equity is built through consistent branding across every touchpoint:

TouchpointWhat It Communicates
PackagingQuality, personality, values
AdvertisingMessage, tone, target audience
Social mediaVoice, engagement, authenticity
Customer serviceCare, reliability, responsiveness
Product designInnovation, attention to detail
Key PointOne bad experience at any touchpoint can damage years of brand building.
Lesson

Dyson — Branding strategy

Dyson built one of the strongest brands in consumer electronics:

Brand ElementHow Dyson Does It
Product designDistinctive industrial look — you recognize a Dyson instantly
PricingAlways premium — signals quality and innovation
Advertising"Say goodbye to the bag" — focused on solving the customer's pain point

Patents~6,000 granted and pending patents — protects innovation from imitation
LeadershipJames Dyson hired a CEO so he could personally focus on design and engineering
Key PointDyson's brand message is consistent: "We engineer things better."
Lesson

Dyson — Brand equity in action

Why do customers pay 2–3x more for a Dyson vacuum?

  • They trust the brand to deliver superior performance
  • The distinctive design signals quality to visitors in their home
  • Years of consistent messaging created perceived value beyond the product
Key Pointequity turns a $150 manufacturing cost into $500 retail

LO 2.4.C — Four stages, four strategies

Part 3: Product Life Cycle

Lesson

What is the product life cycle?

PLC — four stages, launch to withdrawal

Understanding the PLC helps businesses:

  • Anticipate changes in sales and profit
  • Adjust marketing, production, and financial strategy
  • Manage a portfolio of products at different stages
Key Pointnothing sells forever — the question is how long each stage lasts
Lesson

Lesson

Stage 1 — Introduction

What happens:

  • Product just launched — most customers don't know it exists
  • Sales are low and grow slowly
  • Costs are high (R&D, production setup, promotion)
  • The business often operates at a loss

Strategy focus:

  • Build awareness — advertising, demonstrations, free trials
  • Educate early adopters — explain why this product matters
  • Invest heavily in promotion — customers need a reason to try something new
Key PointIntroduction is about convincing the market that your product exists and is worth trying.
Lesson

Stage 2 — Growth

What happens:

  • Sales rise rapidly — word spreads, demand accelerates
  • Profits begin to appear as economies of scale reduce unit costs
  • Competitors enter the market, attracted by visible demand

Strategy focus:

  • Expand distribution — get the product into more stores, channels, regions
  • Differentiate from new competitors — improve features, branding, service
  • Scale production — invest in capacity to meet rising demand
Key PointGrowth is exciting but dangerous — competitors arrive just as the market gets profitable.
Lesson

Stage 3 — Maturity

What happens:

  • Sales reach their peak but growth slows
  • The market is saturated — most potential customers already have the product
  • Competition is heaviest — many competitors fight for the same customers
  • Price wars become common

Strategy focus:

  • Defend market share — loyalty programs, brand reinforcement
  • Optimize costs — improve efficiency to protect margins
  • Product extensions — new features, new colors, limited editions
Key PointMaturity is about holding your position, not expanding it.
Lesson

Stage 4 — Decline

What happens:

  • Sales fall as customer preferences shift or new technology arrives
  • Profits shrink — the business must decide what to do

Three strategic options:

OptionWhen to Choose
Reduce investmentHarvest remaining profits with minimal spending
DiscontinueExit the market and redirect resources
ReinventUpdate the product to start a new life cycle
Exam NoteDecline is not failure — it is a signal to make a strategic decision.
Lesson

Product portfolio management

Smart businesses manage multiple products at different life cycle stages:

ProductPLC StageRole in Portfolio
Product AMaturityCash cow — funds other products
Product BGrowthRising star — needs investment to capture market
Product CIntroductionFuture bet — may succeed or fail
Product DDeclineHarvest or exit
Key Pointspread stages across the line → balances risk

Stimulus

Scenario

Artisan Pops is a small company that makes gourmet ice pops using local fruit.

  • Year 1: Sold at one farmers' market; 120 pops/week; lost money on a rented freezer cart and custom molds
  • Year 2: Added two farmers' markets and a food truck; sales tripled; two copycat brands appeared
  • Year 3: Sold in 15 grocery stores; sales grew 20%; launched a "tropical collection" with mango and passion fruit flavors
  • Year 4: Sales flatlined; grocery stores demanded slotting fees; a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price
Practice Question
Q1Q1
In Year 1, Artisan Pops operated at a loss despite selling 120 pops per week. Which statement best explains why?
AThe company failed to screen its idea before launching
BThe costs of the freezer cart and custom molds exceeded early revenue
CThe company should have launched in grocery stores first
DCustomer demand was too low to support the business
Answer B

Answer: B.

In the introduction stage, fixed costs (equipment, setup) are high and sales volume is low. Revenue from 120 pops/week did not cover the rental and mold costs.

A — Screening happened before launch; the question asks about Year 1 performance
C — Farmers' markets are a common MVP launch channel; there is no evidence grocery stores would have been better
D — 120 pops/week is decent demand; the problem is cost structure, not demand
Key PointIn the introduction stage, losses are normal — high fixed costs + low volume = operating at a deficit.
Practice Question
Q2Q2
In Year 2, two copycat brands appeared. This is most consistent with which stage of the product life cycle?
AIntroduction — competitors test the concept
BGrowth — visible demand attracts new entrants
CMaturity — all competitors are already established
DDecline — competitors try to capture remaining demand
Answer B

Answer: B.

Sales tripled (rapid growth signal), and competitors entered because they could see the demand. This is the hallmark of the growth stage.

A — In introduction, few or no competitors exist; the market is unproven
C — Maturity has heavy existing competition, not new entrants arriving
D — Decline has falling sales, not tripling sales
Key Point"Sales rising fast + competitors appearing" = growth stage every time.
Practice Question
Q3Q3
Artisan Pops launched the "tropical collection" in Year 3. This is an example of:
AProduct development — generating a new idea
BProduct extension — adding variations to a mature product
CBrand repositioning — changing how customers see the brand
DMarket penetration — entering a new geographic market
Answer B

Answer: B.

Adding new flavors (mango, passion fruit) to an existing product line is a classic product extension strategy used during the maturity stage to boost slowing sales.

A — This is not a new product; it is a new flavor of the same product
C — The brand positioning did not change; only the product variety expanded
D — Market penetration means selling more of the same product in the same market; new flavors are product variation, not market expansion
> Product extension = new variations of the same product to re-energize sales.
Practice Question
Q4Q4
In Year 4, grocery stores demanded slotting fees and a new competitor launched organic pops at a lower price. Which PLC stage is Artisan Pops most likely entering?
AIntroduction — still paying to enter the market
BGrowth — expanding distribution with new costs
CMaturity — saturated market, intense competition, margin pressure
DDecline — sales have already fallen to zero
Answer C

Answer: C.

Sales flatlined (saturation), slotting fees squeeze margins, and a competitor is undercutting on price. All three are signals of the maturity stage.

A — Slotting fees are a retailer demand, not a launch cost; the product is already in stores
B — Sales are not rising; "flatlined" ≠ growth
D — Sales have not fallen to zero; they have plateaued. Decline requires falling sales
Key PointFlat sales + margin pressure + price competition = maturity. Falling sales = decline.
Practice Question
Q5Q5
Artisan Pops wants to avoid decline. Which strategy is most appropriate for a product in the late maturity stage?
AIncrease prices to maximize revenue from loyal customers
BReduce investment and harvest remaining profits
CLaunch a new product line to diversify the portfolio while defending the existing brand
DDiscontinue the product before losses begin
Answer C

Answer: C.

Portfolio diversification (new product lines) is the strongest long-term strategy during late maturity. Defending the existing brand while developing new products balances risk.

A — Raising prices in a price-competitive market accelerates customer loss
B — "Harvest" is a decline-stage strategy; in late maturity, there is still time to reinvest
D — Discontinuing a product with still-positive sales is premature; reinvention is better
Exam NoteLate maturity strategy: defend what you have + invest in what comes next.
Practice Question
Q6Q6
A student says: "Dyson's bagless vacuum is in the decline stage because the technology is old." Which statement best evaluates this claim?
ACorrect — old technology always enters decline
BIncorrect — the vacuum is still in the introduction stage because Dyson keeps launching new models
CIncorrect — PLC stage is determined by sales trends and competitive dynamics, not by the age of the technology
DCorrect — Dyson should discontinue the vacuum and focus only on hair products
Answer C

Answer: C.

The PLC stage is determined by sales trends, market saturation, and competitive dynamics — not by how old the underlying technology is. A product with old technology can still be in growth or maturity if sales are strong.

A — Age of technology ≠ PLC stage. Many products use old technology and remain in maturity (e.g., bicycles, umbrellas)
B — New models do not reset the PLC to introduction; they are product extensions within the current stage
D — The vacuum line still generates significant revenue; discontinuing it would be a poor business decision
Key PointPLC stage = what the sales data says, not what the calendar says.

Wrap-up

Wrap-up — 2.4 Product

What we covered:

  • six stages: idea → screen → prototype → concept → MVP → refine
  • Screening (internal) ≠ Concept testing (customer-facing)
  • A prototype explores; an MVP sells
  • Branding builds identity, trust, and pricing power over time through consistency
- Brand equity = the added value from customer recognition and loyalty
  • Product life cycle: Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline
  • Each PLC stage requires different strategies for marketing, production, and finance
  • Product portfolio management balances risk across multiple products
