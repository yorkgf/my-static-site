What is marketing?

Marketing
all the activities a business undertakes to identify customers' problems, needs, and wants, and to promote, sell, and deliver products that meet them.
  • marketing ≠ advertising — ads are one piece
  • studying which bikes sell fast → marketing
  • pricing · posting · adjusting the seat at handoff → marketing
Key Pointcovers the whole journey: understand customer → deliver

Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

CAC
total marketing, advertising, and sales costs used to acquire customers ÷ number of customers acquired (EK 2.1.C.2)

first flea-market month:

  • spends $20 (stall + flyers)
  • 4 buyers arrive through them

CAC = $20 ÷ 4 = $5 per customer

Key Pointreferrals arrive at zero marketing cost → CAC falls
Lesson

CAC — in the examples

Cinder Peak Cocoa — two campaigns:

SpringFall
Advertising costs$6,000$6,000
Other marketing & sales costs$2,000$3,000
New customers acquired400300
Spring CAC = (6,000 + 2,000) ÷ 400 = 8,000 ÷ 400 = $20 · Fall CAC = (6,000 + 3,000) ÷ 300 = $30
Key PointALL costs count — marketing + advertising + sales, never ads alone

Lifetime value (LTV)

LTV
the estimated amount a customer will spend on the business's products over time (EK 2.1.C.3)

track LTV in margin — same units as cost

Renee's LTV:

StepValue
Margin on her first bike$38
2 paid tune-ups × $12 margin$24
sibling bike ~$65 × 40% chance$26

LTV (margin) = 38 + 24 + 26 = $88

Lesson

LTV vs. CAC — the business case

$88 LTV − $5 CAC = $83 net

that gap = the business case for the tune-up, the discount, the photos

Key Pointloyal + repeat customers widen the gap

Data risks — privacy

Key Pointcollecting + storing data can violate privacy — especially without consent
Lesson

Data risks — breach, identity theft, fraud

unsecured data adds a second layer (EK 2.1.D.2):

RiskWhat it means
Data breachExposes stored information to outsiders
Identity theftUses stolen personal details to impersonate a person
FraudUses deception (often powered by stolen data) to take money or property

the whole database = one phone at a flea market — one stolen bag = 19 families exposed

Lesson

Exam points to remember

  • Marketing ≠ advertising — it's identify + promote + sell + deliver; data feeds the who / what / how decisions
  • Demographic (measurable, census-recordable) vs. psychographic (cares about / how they spend time) — use the sorting test
  • segmentation → target → profile — narrow the aim
  • Three relationship tactics: personalized service, rewards programs, customer feedback opportunities
- CAC = ALL acquisition costs ÷ customers acquired (referrals push it toward zero)
- LTV = estimated spending over time — track in margin to compare with cost
  • Data risks: privacy violation, data breach, identity theft, fraud — weigh benefits against trust / core values / reputation
  • Fix pattern: lock → delete → disclose

Scenario

> Riverbend Suds > > Dana Whitfield owns Riverbend Suds, a small-batch soap business with a Saturday farmers market stall and a small online store. Stall shoppers who join her email list get a free sample bar. The store requires an account, and its software logs what each account browses and buys. A short checkout survey asks each customer's age range, zip code, and what matters most when choosing soap. > > Reading the results, Dana sees two clusters: > - Shoppers over 50 who buy single unscented bars and rank sensitive-skin ingredients first. > - Shoppers in their late 20s–30s who buy gift sets, follow the shop on social media, and rank plastic-free packaging first. > > Dana decides her next product line will serve the younger cluster, and drafts a one-page description of an imaginary shopper: Marisol, 31, a renter who hosts friends often, follows zero-waste creators, and buys gifts locally.

Practice Question
Q1Q1
AOnly demographic data, because survey answers can be counted
BBoth demographic data (age + zip code) and psychographic data (what matters most)
COnly psychographic data, because surveys record opinions
DNeither type, because a survey is a traditional tool rather than a digital one
Which best describes the customer data the checkout survey collects?
Answer B

Answer: B.

Demographic = measurable qualities (age, income, location) → age range + zip code

Psychographic = interests, activities, values, lifestyles → "what matters most"

One survey can collect both at once.

A — "measurable" bait. Dana can count how many ranked plastic-free first, but counting never changes what the data describes.
D — confuses the tool with the data type. Type depends on the question asked, never the channel.
Key PointTraits of the person = demographic · Contents of the mind = psychographic.

Q2Q2
AShe can design branding, pricing, and advertising that appeal to anyone who might ever buy soap
BServing one segment guarantees the new line outsells her current products
CShe can stop collecting data from customers outside the chosen segment
DFocusing design, branding, pricing, and advertising on the buyers most likely to purchase is typically more effective and less costly than appealing to a wide audience
Which best explains the advantage Dana gains by designing the next product line for the younger cluster?
Answer D

Answer: D.

Target customer = buyer most likely to purchase because of wants/needs/preferences. Aiming at one group usually converts better and costs less.

A — the opposite strategy. Appealing to anyone who might ever buy is the expensive, unfocused path that targeting exists to replace.
B — "guarantees" is the tell. Targeting improves the odds and promises nothing.

Practice Question
Q4Q4
AIt reduces the cost of manufacturing each unit sold
BIt guarantees members will keep making repeat purchases
CIt builds brand loyalty and repeat purchasing, which raises the lifetime value of each customer
DIt removes any need to attract new customers
Which best explains how a rewards program for frequent buyers can increase a business's profits?
Answer C

Answer: C.

Relationship tactics (rewards, personalized service, feedback) raise LTV because loyal customers make repeat purchases. Satisfied customers also refer friends, pushing CAC down.

B — "guarantees" again. A rewards program raises the probability and promises nothing.
A — borrows from operations. A punch card changes buying behavior and leaves manufacturing cost untouched.
Key PointRelationship tactics move LTV and CAC — never production cost.
