What PESTEL does

These six factors decide which types of businesses are viable in a market.

Key PointViability = the ability to survive and stay successful in a market over time.

Lesson

PPolitical

Policies and political dynamics that affect market activity.

Key tools:

ToolWhat it does
TaxFunds government; often discourages the activity being taxed
SubsidySupports / incentivizes a specific activity
MandateRequires a business to do something
BanProhibits an activity entirely
TariffA tax on imported goods — raises costs for importers
Lesson

PPolitical (examples)

  • A country raising tariffs on imported chocolate → costs rise for every chocolate maker that buys abroad
  • A mandate requiring seat belts in every new car → creates a market no automaker can skip
  • A subsidy for solar panels → incentivizes businesses to install them
  • Political stability matters most where it is missing — businesses avoid markets where the rules might change with the next government
Lesson

PCase study: China's "double reduction" (2021)

One policy decision → an entire industry destroyed overnight.

  • Central government banned for-profit K-12 tutoring → 新东方, 好未来, 高途 stock crashed 90%+
  • 新东方 forced to pivot → 东方甄选 live-stream commerce, 俞敏洪 from educator to live-stream host
  • The company survived — but the original business model is gone
Exam NotePolitical factors don't just nudge — they can rewrite the rules entirely. A ban, a mandate, a subsidy: one decision from authority, and markets appear or disappear.
Lesson

EEconomic

Conditions of the economy itself — not any one company's choices.

Key indicators:

  • Household income
  • Inflation
  • Unemployment
  • Interest rates
  • Economic stability
Lesson

EEconomic (hallway examples)

  • When allowance money shrinks, fewer students have $1.50 to spend → the hallway market shrinks
  • When inflation raises the supermarket price of a bulk candy box → the seller's cost rises before he sells a single bar
Exam NoteExam tip: income, inflation, unemployment, and interest rates all point to the same source — the condition of the economy itself — never to any single company's choices.
Lesson

SSocial

Trends in society and culture that affect consumers.

Key drivers:

  • Demographics — age, income bracket, family size
  • Cultural norms
  • Lifestyle trends
  • Population growth
Lesson

SSocial (hallway examples)

  • A fitness trend sweeps the school → half the buyers switch to protein bars → demand for candy fades without any rival lifting a finger
  • A neighborhood filling with young families changes what sells there
  • A town growing older buys differently than a town filling with students
Key PointDemographics = the measurable traits of a population.
Lesson

SCase study: Labubu goes global (2024)

A social trend created a new product category from nothing.

  • Gen Z collecting culture + "surprise" dopamine + social media unboxing → blind box craze
  • 泡泡玛特's Labubu character went viral in Southeast Asia → fans lining up, resale prices 5-10× retail
  • The product is a toy — but the social trend (collecting, sharing, status signaling) is what created the market
Key PointSocial factors don't respond to what you sell — they shape what people want to buy. A fitness trend doesn't care about your candy; a collecting trend doesn't care about your toy design. Read the culture first.
Lesson

TTechnological

Features of a market tied to the availability of technology.

Key drivers:

  • Internet access
  • Automation of production
  • Pace of innovation
Lesson

TTechnological (hallway examples)

  • The class group chat is technology acting on the hallway market — inventory posted online reaches buyers a backpack cannot
  • A region without reliable internet cannot host an online store
  • A factory that automates can produce at costs a manual rival struggles to match
Key PointThe rate of innovation matters as much as any single tool — markets that adopt technology quickly punish businesses that move slowly.
Lesson

EEnvironmental

External environmental conditions that promote or limit market activity.

Key drivers:

  • Geography
  • Access to renewable / non-renewable resources
  • Waste rules
  • How customers think about the environment
Lesson

EEnvironmental (hallway examples)

  • The heat wave that made cold drinks valuable in 1.2 was an environmental factor doing the selling
  • A drought that ruins a strawberry harvest raises costs for every dessert stand
  • Customers who care about packaging will pay for the business that listens
Key PointGeography counts: a landlocked region cannot host a fishing fleet; a sunny one invites solar energy.
Lesson

LLegal

Specific laws, rules, and regulations that impact market activity.

Key areas:

  • Employment laws
  • Consumer protection laws
  • Health and safety rules
  • Environmental regulations
  • Intellectual property protection
  • Antitrust laws
Lesson

LLegal (hallway examples)

  • A food-handling certificate requirement is a legal factor
  • The patent from 1.2 that protects a medicine is a legal factor
  • Antitrust laws (limit monopolies, block anti-competitive behavior) connect straight back to 1.2
Lesson

Political vs. Legal — the exam's favorite distinction

PPoliticalLLegal
Is aboutBroad policy choicesSpecific laws to comply with
ExamplesTax, subsidy, mandate, banSafety code, licensing requirement, employment law
SourceGovernment decisionCodified rule
> A proposed tax on sugary food = political (policy choice). A law requiring a food-handler license = legal (specific rule).
Lesson

Overlap is real

A single new emissions law can carry political, environmental, and legal weight at once.

Exam NoteThe exam asks for the best fit — and the source of the force decides it.

Classify by the source of the change — not by what it costs, what it touches, or its subject matter.

Lesson

Viability — definition

Viability
the ability to survive and stay successful in a market over time.

The course uses this word constantly — learn it.

Key PointPut the six factors together and they answer one blunt question: what kind of business can survive in this market at all?
Lesson

Viability — what each factor decides

FactorWhat it decides
PPoliticalWhich activities are incentivized vs. disincentivized
EEconomicHow much customers, businesses, and government can spend
SSocialWhat people want to buy
TTechnologicalWhat can be produced, distributed, communicated
EEnvironmentalWhat resources are even available
LLegalThe cost of operating + the line around what's allowed
Key PointMost businesses need a strong economy, but some thrive in a weak one — like discount stores when incomes fall.
Lesson

Applying the framework — the method

This is the skill the exam actually tests — using PESTEL to evaluate a market.

  1. Identify which factors are relevant to your product
  2. Assess how each one affects the market's attractiveness and its risks
  3. A business enters where the factors align with its model, resources, and customers
Key PointA tech company locates where internet and electricity are cheap. A farm-to-table restaurant opens where customers value local food. Same framework, opposite conclusions.

Lesson

How to write a strong exam answer

Name the factor → state its direction → tie it to this specific business.

  • A factor that's good news for one model is bad news for another
  • Cheap electricity attracts a data center; it does nothing for a tutoring service
  • The fitness trend helps a protein bar seller; it hurts the candy seller

Lesson

When factors change

PESTEL factors move — and when they move they can rewrite an existing market overnight.

  • Flu season empties the school → every hallway sale disappears for two weeks
  • A new law, a downturn, a technology shift — each redraws the map for businesses already operating
  • The memo that opened this video was one changed factor, and it ended a market two competitors had spent weeks fighting over
Exam NoteExam signal: when a question describes a market shifting after a new law, a downturn, or a technology jump, it's testing whether you can name which factor moved and what it did to viability.
Lesson

Exam points to remember

  • Rivals are inside the market; PESTEL factors press from outside
- Viability = survive and stay successful over time
  • Classify by the source of the change — not by what it costs or touches
  • Political vs. legal: broad policy choice vs. specific rule to follow
  • Overlap is real — pick the best fit by the source
  • Economic factors describe the whole economy, never one company
- "All six equally important" = wrong answer

Stimulus

Scenario

Elena Reyes owns GreenBox, a meal-prep delivery service in a mid-size city. Three developments have her attention:

1. The city council is debating a proposal to tax sugary packaged foods 2. A fitness lifestyle trend is growing among adults; gym memberships have risen for three straight years 3. A new state law will require every food delivery business to hold a certified food-handler license within one year

Elena is deciding whether to expand GreenBox into two nearby towns.

Practice Question
Q1Classify the fitness trend

The growing fitness lifestyle trend and rising gym memberships are best classified as which type of PESTEL factor?

AEconomic
BSocial
CTechnological
DPolitical

Pause. Ask: what is the source of this change — the economy, or what people choose to value?

-->

Answer B

Why

Social factors are trends in society and culture that affect consumers, including lifestyle trends. A fitness trend that changes what people want to eat is a lifestyle trend acting on consumer demand.

A. Rising gym memberships involve people spending money, and spending sounds economic. But economic factors describe the condition of the economy itself** (income, inflation, unemployment).
Exam NoteMaster rule: classify by the source of the change. A change in what people choose to value is social, even when the choice costs money.
Practice Question
Q2The paired-concept swap

The proposed tax on sugary packaged foods and the new food-handler license requirement are best classified, respectively, as which two PESTEL factors?

ALegal, then political
BPolitical, then environmental
CEconomic, then legal
DPolitical, then legal
Answer D

Why

A proposed tax is a policy choice → political. A specific law requiring a license → legal.

A — swaps the pair.** The sorting rule: political = broad policy choice (tax/ban); legal = specific rule to follow (license/safety code).
C — a tax affects money, but its source is a government policy decision**, and source decides the classification.
Practice Question
Q3How to use the framework

Which best describes how Elena should use the PESTEL framework in her expansion decision?

AAdopt the framework only after choosing which town to enter
BWeigh only the factors that favor expansion
CIdentify the relevant factors and assess how each affects the attractiveness and risks of each town
DTreat all six factors as equally important in every market
Answer C

Why

The framework works by identifying factors relevant to the specific product and market, then assessing how each one impacts attractiveness and risk. The license law is a cost in every town; the fitness trend may be stronger in one town than another.

D. A scenario will rarely make all six matter equally** — relevance depends on the business. A delivery service cares about a licensing law; a candy stand doesn't care about internet speeds.
Warning"Treat every factor as equally weighted" sounds thorough and reads as a wrong answer.

Q4What does the data support?

A business selling premium home fitness equipment is comparing the two regions. Based on the economic factors shown, which conclusion is supported by the table?

ARegion North currently offers the more attractive economic conditions for a premium product

Practice Question
Q5Solar-panel subsidies

A country's government begins offering large subsidies to businesses that install solar panels. Which statement best describes this in PESTEL terms?

AA legal factor, because subsidies are written into law
BAn environmental factor, because solar panels affect the environment
CA political factor that incentivizes a specific business activity
DA technological factor, because solar panels are technology
Answer C

Why

Subsidies are policy tools (alongside taxes, mandates, bans); their purpose is to incentivize an activity the government wants more of → political.

B — classify by the source of the force**, never by the subject matter it touches. The subsidy concerns solar panels, but the force acting on the market is a government policy choice.
Practice Question
Q6Fiber internet reaches rural areas

A homework-help app operates where most households have high-speed internet. A new fiber network then brings fast internet to rural areas for the first time. Which statement best describes the effect?

AIt raises a barrier to entry that protects the app from rivals
BA technological factor changed, expanding the app's viable market
CIt converts the app's service from a want into a need
DIt is a social factor, because more people can now join online communities
Answer B

Why

Internet access is a technological factor. When it spreads, the set of customers a technology-dependent business can serve grows with it — a market that was out of reach becomes viable.

A — borrows Topic 1.2 vocabulary. A barrier to entry keeps new firms out, and wider internet access is the opposite: it opens the market to the app and to future rivals alike.**
Key PointRecognizing a term from a neighboring topic is never enough — the scenario has to match what the term means.
