Practice — 6 questions

Read → pause → commit to an answer → then reveal.
3 on one stimulus (Osei Printworks)· 3 standalone
Stimulus

Scenario

Marcus Osei owns Osei Printworks, a small print shop with two employees. Posted core values: quality and honesty. A rush order of 500 event programs comes back with a printing flaw visible only under bright light. Reprinting costs $300 and misses the customer's deadline. A new employee suggests shipping the programs without mentioning the flaw. Marcus is also drafting the shop's first code of conduct and is considering a bonus that pays employees for each rush order completed on schedule.

> Watch for the artifacts: a concealment suggestion, an incentive (the bonus), and a potential modeling moment.

Practice Question
Q1Classify the employee's suggestion

The employee's suggestion to ship the programs without mentioning the flaw is best described as which?

AConcealing information from a customer, a form of unethical behavior
BMisusing company property
CAn ethical dilemma between two core values
DA legal factor affecting the print market

Pause — does the question ask about Marcus's dilemma, or about the employee's suggestion?

Answer A

Why

Shipping a product with a known flaw and saying nothing means the customer pays believing something the seller knows is false — concealing information is a named form of unethical behavior.

C — Marcus does face a genuine dilemma (honesty vs. deadline), but the question asks about the employee's specific suggestion — and that suggestion is silence for gain, which is simply unethical behavior.
Key PointClassify the specific action described. A dilemma can exist in the background while the question asks about concealment.
Practice Question
Q2Classify the on-schedule bonus

The proposed bonus for each rush order completed on schedule is best described as which?

AA code of conduct provision
BAn internal repercussion for unethical behavior
CAn incentive structure that could influence employees toward cutting corners
DModeling ethical behavior for the staff
Answer C

Why

An incentive is a reward that encourages a particular action. A bonus tied only to on-schedule completion rewards speed and pays the same whether the flaw is disclosed or hidden — so it quietly leans on employees to ship and stay silent.

B — points the concept backward. A repercussion is a consequence imposed after unethical behavior; the bonus arrives before any behavior and rewards output.
Key PointSort by direction: incentives pull toward behavior; repercussions push back after it.
Practice Question
Q3Marcus reprints + explains + shows the note

Marcus reprints the order at the shop's expense, tells both employees why, and shows them the note he sent the customer. This action best represents which?

AAn external repercussion
BModeling ethical behavior
COutsourcing an ethical decision
DA conflict of interest
Answer B

Why

Modeling means leaders demonstrate the ethical behavior themselves, so employees learn the standard by watching it — Marcus performed the standard in front of his staff at real cost.

A — a repercussion is a consequence aimed at someone who behaved unethically, and nobody here is being punished. Marcus absorbed a cost to demonstrate a value — that's modeling.
Key PointWhen a question shows a leader acting (rather than rewarding or punishing), look for modeling.
Practice Question
Q4Stakeholder classification

Which best identifies a correct classification of stakeholders in a business?

ACustomers are internal; owners are external
BManagers are external; community members are internal
COwners are internal; employees are external
DEmployees are internal; government agencies are external
Answer D

Why

Internal = direct involvement in operations/decisions/outcomes (owners, managers, employees). External = vested interest without being employed or directly involved (customers, government agencies, community members).

C — gets owners right and then exports the employees to external.
Exam NoteThe sorting rule is direct involvement — importance and seniority play no part. An employee works inside daily operations → internal alongside owners/managers.
Practice Question
Q5Reading a benefit-and-cost table

A furniture maker must decide whether to switch to a cheaper overseas supplier whose delivery delays would reach customers. Estimated effects:

OptionInternal stakeholdersExternal stakeholders
Keep current−$90,000 (higher input prices)+$70,000 (reliable deliveries)
Switch+$90,000 (savings)−$150,000 (late/canceled orders)

Study the numbers. What moved? What didn't?

Practice Question
Q5What does the data support?

Using the approach of greatest total benefit or least total harm across all stakeholders, which decision does the table support?

ASwitching — it produces a $90,000 benefit for internal stakeholders
BSwitching — customer losses are not costs to the company
CKeeping — its net cost of $20,000 is smaller than the $60,000 net cost of switching
DKeeping — reliable suppliers always increase long-run profits
Answer C

Work each option across all stakeholders:

- Keep: +$70,000 − $90,000 = net cost of $20,000
- Switch: +$90,000 − $150,000 = net cost of $60,000

Both options hurt — so the method picks the least total harm → $20,000 is the smaller loss.

A — totals one column and stops. The method exists precisely to count every stakeholder group.
D — reaches the right decision with a broken reason — and "always" is the tell. No ethical choice carries a promised profit outcome.
Key PointAlways sum across every stakeholder column before totaling.
Practice Question
Q6How a response to a lapse hits profitability

Which best explains how a business's response to an ethical lapse can affect its profitability?

AProfitability is unaffected unless a law was broken
BThe response shapes customer relationships, employee relationships, and public perception, which in turn affect revenues and costs
CA sincere apology guarantees customers will return
DEthical responses matter only for large corporations with public brands
Answer B

Why

The chain runs response → relationships and perception → profitability. Customers reward trust with loyalty and punish deception by leaving; employees weigh employers the same way; both movements land on the revenue and cost lines.

A — borrows legal-factor thinking from Topic 1.3. Reputational consequences can arise whether or not any law is involved.
C — fails on "guarantees." A good response improves the odds and promises nothing.
Key PointReputation needs no law. A guarantee dressed as reasoning fails even when its verdict happens to match.
