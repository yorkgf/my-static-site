Lesson

What is unethical behavior?

Unethical behavior
actions that violate moral or professional standards.

The CED names three forms:

🤐

Falsifying or concealing

Lying on a label, hiding a known flaw, not disclosing a defect.

💼

Misusing company property

Using business resources for personal gain without authorization.

⚠️

Causing harm

Ignoring safety standards, selling a product known to be dangerous.

Lesson

Concealing information — the snack box test

Key PointThe helper's suggestion — hide the expired chips — is concealing information.

It doesn't matter that the bag is small. The customer is paying for something the seller knows is false.

Classify the action itself: the helper did not create a dilemma — the helper suggested hiding a known defect for financial gain. That is unethical behavior.

Lesson

Where does it come from?

Unethical behavior can occur at all levels of a business — owner, manager, or entry-level employee.

  • The source doesn't change the classification
  • The behavior is what the exam asks you to identify
  • A manager hiding a safety report is the same form as a cashier hiding an expired bag
Lesson

Incentive structures — the hidden driver

One common driver of unethical behavior: incentive structures.

Key PointAn incentive = a reward that encourages a particular action.

When an incentive rewards output without checking how the output was produced, it can quietly push people toward unethical choices that benefit themselves.

Lesson

Incentive structures — the snack box test

Key PointIf the snack box paid a bonus for every Saturday with zero unsold inventory, what would the helper learn to do with the last few bags?

The incentive rewards zero leftover — it doesn't ask how the helper achieved it. The helper learns to hide or discard expiring bags rather than report them.

Key PointThe incentive didn't say "cheat." It said "produce results" — and the system never checked the method. That's how incentives quietly create unethical behavior.
Lesson

How businesses encourage ethical behavior

Four tools the exam tests — each works differently:

BEFORE

📜 Code of conduct

A written document outlining expected behavior and standards.

BEFORE

🎓 Training

Teaching employees to follow ethical guidelines in real situations.

AFTER

⚡ Internal repercussions

Consequences imposed after a transgression (warnings, suspension, termination).

CONTINUOUSLY

👀 Modeling ethical behavior

Leaders demonstrate the standard — employees learn by watching.

Key PointSort them by when they act: codes and training arrive before any behavior; repercussions arrive after; modeling runs continuously.
Lesson

Code of conduct

A code of conduct is a written document that defines the business's ethical expectations.

  • Sets clear boundaries employees can reference
  • Often covers conflicts of interest, confidentiality, customer treatment
  • Is only as strong as its enforcement
Key PointThe snack box writes on the cooler lid: "Check every date. If it's past, pull it — no exceptions." That's a one-line code of conduct.
Lesson

Training

Training teaches employees how to apply ethical guidelines in real situations.

  • Moves ethics from a poster on the wall to practice scenarios
  • Helps employees recognize dilemmas before they act
  • Must be ongoing, not one-time
Key PointMarcus (the print shop owner in the practice questions) holds a monthly fifteen-minute session on handling rush-order defects. That's training.
Lesson

Internal repercussions

An internal repercussion = a consequence imposed after an ethical transgression.

  • Can range from a written warning to termination
  • Exists to discourage repeat behavior
  • Must be consistent and proportional to be credible
Key PointDirection test: a repercussion pushes backward — it arrives after the behavior and deters the next occurrence.
Lesson

Modeling ethical behavior

Modeling
a leader demonstrates ethical behavior in front of employees, so the standard is learned by observation, not instruction.
  • Works because employees watch what leaders do, not just what they say
  • Most powerful when it costs the leader something (time, money, short-term revenue)
Exam NoteMarcus reprints the flawed order at his own expense, tells both employees why, and shows them the customer note. That's modeling — the value survived contact with a real cost.
Lesson

Incentives vs. repercussions — the direction test

The pair the exam loves to swap. Sort by direction.

⬇️ Pulls toward · BEFORE

🟢 Incentive

A reward that encourages a particular action.

Example: Bonus for on-schedule completion.

Arrives before the behavior.

⬆️ Pushes back · AFTER

🔴 Repercussion

A consequence imposed after an ethical transgression.

Example: Written warning for concealing a defect.

Arrives after the behavior.

Key PointA bonus that rewards speed without checking disclosure is an incentive that leans on employees to cut corners.

Read each scenario. Decide: code of conduct, training, repercussion, or modeling?
Lesson

Why ethics matter — the forward chain

Ethical business practices attract both customers and employees and contribute to brand loyalty.

The chain runs:

Key PointEthical behavior → trust from customers + employees → loyalty → higher revenue and lower costs
Key PointCustomers reward trust with loyalty. Employees who trust the employer stay longer and work harder. Both effects improve profitability.
Lesson

Why ethics matter — the reverse chain

And in reverse:

Key PointUnethical behavior (or a weak response to it) → lost trust → customers leave, employees disengage → lower revenue and higher costs
Key PointA business's response to an ethical lapse shapes customer relationships, employee relationships, and public perception — all of which land on the revenue and cost lines.

Reputation needs no law — customers rarely wait for a court ruling before they stop buying.

Lesson

What is an ethical dilemma?

Ethical dilemma
a situation where a core value (transparency, fairness, empathy) conflicts with another core value or with a business goal or practice.
  • Both sides have a legitimate claim — that's what makes it a dilemma
  • The question is always: "What should I do?" — not "Did someone do something wrong?"
Lesson

The snack box dilemma

Exam NoteThe snack box faces a dilemma: honesty (tell the family the chips are past date) conflicts with the business goal (sell everything, maximize Saturday revenue).
  • Honesty side: the family deserves to know what they're buying
  • Revenue goal side: the business needs to sell inventory to stay profitable
Key PointBoth sides have a legitimate claim. That's what makes it a dilemma — there is no cost-free answer.
Lesson

Dilemma vs. unethical behavior

A dilemma is not the same as unethical behavior.

Ethical dilemmaUnethical behavior
Trade-offsBoth sides have legitimate claimsOne side is clearly wrong
ExampleHonesty vs. revenue goalConcealing a known defect
The question"What should I do?""Did someone do something wrong?"
Lesson

The distinction matters — exam trap

Key PointConcealing the expired chips is not a dilemma — it's choosing the wrong side.

The dilemma existed before the helper spoke: honesty vs. revenue. The helper's suggestion — hide the defect — is unethical behavior, not a dilemma.

Lesson

The sorting rule

Key PointIf one side is clearly wrong, it's unethical behavior. If both sides have legitimate claims, it's a dilemma.

Sort by this rule, not by how uncomfortable the situation feels.

  • Clearly wrong → unethical behavior (no trade-off, just a bad choice)
  • Legitimate claims on both sides → ethical dilemma (real trade-off, no cost-free answer)
Lesson

Classify the situation — exam habit

When the exam gives you a scenario, sort it first:

What you seeIt is…
A leader faces two competing values, or a value vs. a goalAn ethical dilemma
Someone lies, hides, or takes somethingUnethical behavior
Someone suggests hiding a flaw for gainUnethical behavior (concealing information) — even if a dilemma exists elsewhere in the scenario
Key PointClassify the specific action described. A dilemma can exist in the background while the question asks about a specific act of concealment.
Lesson

Stakeholders — who has a stake?

Stakeholder
any individual or group affected by a business's decisions.

Two categories — sorted by direct involvement.

🏛️ Internal

Direct involvement in operations, decisions, and outcomes.

Owners · managers · employees

🌐 External

Not directly employed or involved, but have a vested interest.

Customers · government · community members

Sorting ruleDirect involvement, not importance or seniority. Even the most loyal customer remains external.
Lesson

The sorting rule — direct involvement

Exam NoteThe sorting rule is direct involvement — importance and seniority play no part.
  • An employee sweeps the floor daily → internal alongside owners and managers
  • A customer who buys every week → still external — they have a vested interest but no role in operations
  • The government sets the rules → external — it is affected by the business but not employed by it
Key PointEven the most loyal customer remains external. Importance ≠ involvement.
Lesson

Two lenses for resolving an ethical dilemma

Lens 1

Stakeholder arithmetic

  • Evaluate benefits and costs of each option for every stakeholder group (internal + external)
  • Choose the option producing the greatest total benefit or least total harm
  • A net impact calculation — weigh all groups, not just the loudest one
Lens 2

Vision and goals

  • Choose the option most consistent with the business's vision and/or goals
  • If the vision already contains the value, the answer may be built in
  • "Game days where no family scrambles" — selling expired chips scrambles trust.
Exam NoteBoth lenses agree? Strong answer. Conflict? The exam prompt signals which to lead with — read the question stem.
Lesson

When the two lenses conflict

Exam NoteWhen both lenses point the same direction, the case is strong. When they conflict, the exam will tell you which lens to prioritize.
  • If Lens 1 and Lens 2 agree → strong answer, no conflict
  • If they disagree → the exam prompt will signal which lens to lead with
Lesson

Which lens to use — the decision tree

  • If the exam says "consider the stakeholders" → use Lens 1 (stakeholder arithmetic)
  • If the exam says "consistent with the vision" → use Lens 2
  • If the exam gives both → both matter, but the vision is the tiebreaker
Lesson

The question stem is your instruction manual

Exam NoteDon't guess the priority — read the question. The exam prompt will signal which lens to lead with.

The question stem tells you what the examiners want to see. If it says "evaluate the impact on stakeholders," they want Lens 1. If it says "consistent with the company's vision," they want Lens 2.

WarningWrong lens = wrong answer, even if your reasoning is correct. Always start by reading the question.
Lesson

Stakeholder arithmetic — worked example

A furniture maker considers switching to a cheaper overseas supplier whose delays would reach customers.

OptionInternal stakeholdersExternal stakeholdersNet
Keep current−$90,000 (higher input prices)+$70,000 (reliable deliveries)−$20,000
Switch+$90,000 (savings)−$150,000 (late/canceled orders)−$60,000

Both options hurt — so the method picks the least total harm → keep current.

Key PointAlways sum across every stakeholder column. An option that pays insiders by costing outsiders more is exactly what the arithmetic is built to catch.

Case study — Ctrip (携程)

When a business has the data to charge loyal customers more — should it?
Lesson

Ctrip — the dilemma

Ctrip (携程) is China's largest online travel agency — hotel bookings, flights, train tickets, vacation packages. Market share exceeds 50%.

The controversy: 大数据杀熟 — using data analytics to charge loyal, frequent users higher prices than new users for the same product, same room, same flight.

Lesson

Ctrip — how 大数据杀熟 works

The algorithm analyzes each user's behavior — booking frequency, device type, search history, price sensitivity — to determine their maximum willingness to pay.

Key PointA loyal user who books 20 trips/year sees ¥500 for a hotel room. A first-time user searching for the same room, same night sees ¥380.

The algorithm knows the loyal user is less likely to switch — so it charges more.

Key PointIs this smart pricing — or exploiting trust? That's the dilemma.
Lesson

Ctrip — the two sides

Core value: fairness + transparencyBusiness goal: profit maximization
Loyal customers should be rewarded, not penalizedDynamic pricing is standard in airlines and hotels
Users don't know they're seeing a different price — no transparencyData analytics lets the company match price to willingness-to-pay
Exploiting trust destroys brand loyalty over timeHigher revenue per user → more profit → more investment
Lesson

Ctrip — why this is a genuine dilemma

Key PointThis is a genuine dilemma: the business has a legal right to set prices, but exercising that right in this way conflicts with fairness and transparency.
  • Legally, Ctrip is allowed to charge different prices to different users
  • Ethically, loyal customers are being penalized for their loyalty — without knowing it
  • Neither side is frivolous — that's the exam's threshold for a "genuine" dilemma
Lesson

Ctrip — stakeholder arithmetic

Ask: who benefits and who is harmed by 大数据杀熟?

StakeholderImpact
InternalCtrip / investors+ Higher revenue per loyal user
ExternalLoyal customers− Pay more for the same product; feel deceived
ExternalNew customers+ Pay less (subsidized by loyal users)
ExternalCompetitors (Meituan, Fliggy)+ Loyal users who discover the practice may switch
Lesson

Ctrip — short-term vs. long-term

Key PointShort-term: internal stakeholders benefit — higher revenue per loyal user, no immediate cost.
Key PointLong-term: trust erodes, loyal customers leave, brand reputation suffers — the revenue gain is temporary.

When users discover the practice (and they did — screenshots went viral on Weibo), the loyal users who felt betrayed switched to competitors. The short-term profit came at the cost of the company's most valuable asset: repeat customers.

Lesson

Ctrip — the resolution question

Pair up. 2 minutes.

If you were Ctrip's CEO, which approach would you take?

  1. Keep 大数据杀熟 — it's legal, it maximizes short-term revenue, and most users never find out
  2. Stop the practice — reward loyalty with lower prices, sacrifice short-term revenue for long-term trust
  3. Transparent dynamic pricing — show all users the same base price, but offer loyalty discounts openly
Key PointLens 1: stakeholder arithmetic — what produces the greatest total benefit or least total harm? Lens 2: consistency with vision — what would a company that says "make travel easier" do?
Lesson

The absolutes trap

Watch for "always," "never," and "guarantee" in answer choices:

  • "Reliable suppliers always increase long-run profits" → reaches the right decision with a broken reason — and "always" is the tell.
  • "A sincere apology guarantees customers will return" → a good response improves the odds and promises nothing.
Key PointA right verdict reached by a guaranteed-profit reason is still wrong.
Lesson

The legal-factor trap

Watch for answer choices that borrow from Topic 1.3 (PESTEL):

  • "Profitability is unaffected unless a law was broken" → legal consequences are one risk; reputational consequences arise whether or not any law is involved.
  • Customers rarely wait for a court ruling before they stop buying.
Key PointReputation needs no law.

Stimulus

Scenario

Marcus Osei owns Osei Printworks, a small print shop with two employees. Posted core values: quality and honesty. A rush order of 500 event programs comes back with a printing flaw visible only under bright light. Reprinting costs $300 and misses the customer's deadline. A new employee suggests shipping the programs without mentioning the flaw. Marcus is also drafting the shop's first code of conduct and is considering a bonus that pays employees for each rush order completed on schedule.

> Watch for the artifacts: a concealment suggestion, an incentive (the bonus), and a potential modeling moment.

Practice Question
Q1Classify the employee's suggestion

The employee's suggestion to ship the programs without mentioning the flaw is best described as which?

AConcealing information from a customer, a form of unethical behavior
BMisusing company property
CAn ethical dilemma between two core values
DA legal factor affecting the print market

Pause. Ask: does the question ask about the dilemma Marcus faces, or the specific suggestion the employee makes?

-->

Answer A

Why

Shipping a product with a known flaw and saying nothing means the customer pays believing something the seller knows is false — concealing information is a named form of unethical behavior.

C — Marcus does face a genuine dilemma (honesty vs. deadline), but the question asks about the employee's specific suggestion — and that suggestion is silence for gain**, which is simply unethical behavior.
Key PointClassify the specific action described. A dilemma can exist in the background while the question asks about concealment.
Practice Question
Q2Classify the on-schedule bonus

The proposed bonus for each rush order completed on schedule is best described as which?

AA code of conduct provision
BAn internal repercussion for unethical behavior
CAn incentive structure that could influence employees toward cutting corners
DModeling ethical behavior for the staff
Answer C

Why

An incentive is a reward that encourages a particular action. A bonus tied only to on-schedule completion rewards speed and pays the same whether the flaw is disclosed or hidden — so it quietly leans on employees to ship and stay silent.

B — points the concept backward. A repercussion is a consequence imposed after unethical behavior; the bonus arrives before** any behavior and rewards output.
Key PointSort by direction: incentives pull toward behavior; repercussions push back after it.
Practice Question
Q3Marcus reprints + explains + shows the note

Marcus reprints the order at the shop's expense, tells both employees why, and shows them the note he sent the customer. This action best represents which?

AAn external repercussion
BModeling ethical behavior
COutsourcing an ethical decision
DA conflict of interest
Answer B

Why

Modeling means leaders demonstrate the ethical behavior themselves, so employees learn the standard by watching it — Marcus performed the standard in front of his staff at real cost.

A — a repercussion is a consequence aimed at someone who behaved unethically, and nobody here is being punished. Marcus absorbed a cost to demonstrate a value** — that's modeling.
Key PointWhen a question shows a leader acting (rather than rewarding or punishing), look for modeling.

BManagers are external; community members are internal
COwners are internal; employees are external

Practice Question
Q5What does the data support?

Using the approach of greatest total benefit or least total harm across all stakeholders, which decision does the table support?

ASwitching — it produces a $90,000 benefit for internal stakeholders
BSwitching — customer losses are not costs to the company
CKeeping — its net cost of $20,000 is smaller than the $60,000 net cost of switching
DKeeping — reliable suppliers always increase long-run profits
Answer C

Work each option across all stakeholders:

- Keep: +$70,000 − $90,000 = net cost of $20,000
- Switch: +$90,000 − $150,000 = net cost of $60,000

Both options hurt — so the method picks the least total harm → $20,000 is the smaller loss.

A — totals one column and stops. The method exists precisely to count every stakeholder group.**
D — reaches the right decision with a broken reason — and "always" is the tell.** No ethical choice carries a promised profit outcome.
Key PointAlways sum across every stakeholder column before totaling.

AProfitability is unaffected unless a law was broken

Why
