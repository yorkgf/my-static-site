1.5 Vision

Lesson deck — concepts + practice
Lesson

Today's goals

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Distinguish between core values and core competencies, and explain how each shapes business and personal decisions
  2. Evaluate vision statements and mission statements — and tell them apart
  3. Classify organizations as businesses, social enterprises, or nonprofits

Running story continues: the snack box is three weeks old, and two offers arrive in the same week that the cash box can't decide between.

Lesson

The two offers

The snack box has regulars — then one week brings two offers:

  1. A parent offers to bankroll a candy-only mega cart: double the inventory, double the margin — but the fruit the families asked for would have to go
  2. The league asks him to take over the far field's snack table — a bigger job that pays less at first and runs entirely on trust

One pair of hands, one Saturday, two doors.

Key PointNothing in his cash box answers this, because it's a decision about what the business is for and what it is best at — not about the arithmetic.
Lesson

Core values — definition

Core values
the defining beliefs and principles that guide an individual's or a business's actions.

Examples: creativity, excellence, transparency, empathy, reliability.

He writes three on the lid of his cooler:

  • Show up on time
  • Listen to the families
  • Keep prices fair
Lesson

Why communicate core values?

Businesses communicate core values for two reasons:

  1. Align employees to a shared purpose
  2. Guide decision makers toward choices consistent with what the business believes
Key PointHis cousin helps on Saturdays, and the cooler lid is how the cousin makes the right call when nobody's watching. That's the job values do: turn one person's judgment into the whole team's default.
Lesson

Testing a stated value

Run the mega cart past the lid: double margin, but the fruit goes — and the families asked for that fruit two weeks ago.

The cart fails "listen to the families" before the math even starts.

Key PointA value the business abandons the first time money appears was a decoration. Test any stated value by asking: what did it cost the business at the last real decision?
  • Transparency → what goes on the label?
  • Empathy → how a refund is handled?
  • Excellence → whether a flawed batch ships?
Lesson

Core competencies — definition

Core competencies
the capabilities, skills, and expertise that help an individual or a business outperform rivals and achieve competitive advantage.

Examples: innovation, customer service, communication, ethical behavior, efficiency.

His list:

  • The pre-order system in the group chat (nobody else takes orders before game day)
  • The halftime handoff (one minute per team)
  • A reputation for showing up (carried from the hallway in 1.1)
Lesson

Core value vs. core competency

The pair the exam loves to swap — pin the difference now:

💛 What it believes

VCore value

A defining belief or principle that guides the business's actions.

Signal: "Believes"

e.g. reliability on the cooler lid

🔧 What it's good at

CCore competency

A capability or skill that helps it outperform rivals.

Signal: "Good at"

e.g. the 60-second handoff

Key PointA value is what the business believes; a competency is what it is good at. Speed is a built skill → competency.
Lesson

Competencies feed competitive advantage

Competitive advantage (from 1.2) = the edge that lets one win customers from a rival.

Core competencies feed that edge. A differentiated product, a lower cost, a faster service — trace an advantage backward and you usually find a capability somebody developed.

Exam NoteRule of thumb: pursue the opportunities your strengths can win, and spend resources where those strengths compound.

The far-field table leans on his logistics + reputation; the mega cart leans mostly on someone else's money.

Lesson

Values and competencies for individuals

The same tool points at you. Individuals consider their own core values and core competencies when choosing educational paths, careers, and jobs.

  • A student who values independence and is skilled with systems reads one set of options
  • A student who values service and is skilled with people reads another
Key PointTwo job offers: one pays 20% more but demands weekend hours that collide with something you refuse to give up; the other pays less but builds a skill already on your strengths list.
Key PointKnowing your list before the offer arrives is the entire advantage.
Lesson

Vision statement — definition

Vision statement
a concise description of the future the business is trying to create — its aspirations and core values.
Key PointHis vision: "Game days where no family scrambles."
  • Describes no operations — just a future worth wanting
  • The aspiration, not the work
Lesson

Mission statement — definition

Mission statement = describes what the business does and how it will achieve its long-term goals.
Key PointHis mission: "Deliver pre-ordered snack boxes to every team by halftime, reliably, at prices families can trust."
  • Describes the work — what it does + how
  • The operation, not the aspiration
Lesson

Vision vs. mission — the test

Test any statement with one question: does it describe a future worth wanting, or does it describe the work?

🌅 Aspiration

●Vision statement

The future the business is trying to create.

Signal: "Aspires" — a future worth wanting.

No operations. Just a destination.

🛠️ Operation

●Mission statement

What the business does and how it does it.

Signal: "Does and how" — describes the work.

The operation, not the aspiration.

Lesson

Classify by content — not by label

Key Point"A city where every student graduates ready" → no operations, all aspiration → vision. "We provide afterschool math tutoring in three neighborhoods, priced by family income" → what it does + how → mission.

The labels are unreliable: the words "vision" and "mission" rarely appear inside the statements themselves — classify by content.

Lesson

Why vision and mission matter — internally

These statements earn their space twice. Inside the business:

  • Communicate long-term goals + core values
  • Guide decision making
  • Give everyone a shared purpose
Exam NoteThe cooler-lid test: the snack box's vision ("Game days where no family scrambles") tells the cousin what to do when nobody's watching — that's the internal job.
Lesson

Why vision and mission matter — externally

Outside the business, vision and mission statements earn trust before a single transaction:

  • Customers read the mission and learn what the business stands for without buying first
  • Investors and partners evaluate alignment with their own goals before committing resources
  • Community members gauge whether the business shares their values
Key PointThe mission does the work before the first sale.
Lesson

Vision and mission — the snack box test

Key PointWhen the wholesale parent reads the snack box's mission — "Deliver pre-ordered snack boxes to every team by halftime, reliably, at prices families can trust" — he learns the fruit stays.

He doesn't need to buy a single bag first. He doesn't need to watch a Saturday.

The statement told him what he needed to know.

Lesson

The external job — mission before the first sale

Key PointThat's the external job: the mission answers the question a customer or partner would otherwise have to ask by experience.

A parent who reads the mission knows the fruit stays — without buying a single bag.

An investor who reads the vision knows the direction — without sitting in a single meeting.

The mission does the work before the first transaction.

Case study — Patagonia

When a company actually lives its values.
Lesson

Patagonia — the statements

Founded 1973 by Yvon Chouinard — a climber who built gear for friends.

StatementContentType
●Vision"We're in business to save our home planet."Aspiration — no operations, a future worth wanting
●Mission"Build the best product, cause no unnecessary harm, use business to inspire and implement solutions to the environmental crisis."What + how — the work itself
Key PointRun the test: one sentence describes a future; the other describes the work.
Lesson

Patagonia — transparency tested

  • Transparency → published its own supply-chain failures publicly before anyone asked

The company released a report admitting that some suppliers used exploitative labor practices — then published its own plan to fix them. Most companies hide supply-chain problems; Patagonia volunteered them.

Key PointThe cost: short-term reputation risk. The gain: long-term trust.
Lesson

Patagonia — environmental commitment tested

  • Environmental commitment → ran a full-page ad on Black Friday: "Don't Buy This Jacket" — telling customers to buy less

On the biggest shopping day of the year, Patagonia told consumers not to buy its products unless they truly needed them. The ad listed the environmental cost of producing the jacket.

Key PointThe cost: revenue from the biggest sales day of the year. The gain: a brand identity no competitor could copy.
Lesson

Patagonia — quality as a value

  • Quality → offered free lifetime repairs, sacrificing repeat purchases
Key PointThe value said "build things that last" — not "build things that break on schedule so customers come back."

A jacket that lasts fifteen years costs Patagonia fifteen years of replacement sales. The value held anyway.

Lesson

The pattern — values survived contact with revenue

Three tests. Three values. Every one cost money.

ValueDecisionRevenue impact
TransparencyPublished own supply-chain failuresShort-term reputation risk
Environmental commitment"Don't Buy This Jacket" adTold customers not to buy
QualityFree lifetime repairsSacrificed repeat purchases
Key PointTest any stated value by asking: what did it cost the business at the last real decision?
Lesson

The principle — values vs. money

Key PointA value the business abandons the first time money appears was a decoration.

Patagonia's values survived contact with revenue across every test — transparency, environmental commitment, and quality.

Ask yourself: does your snack box's cooler-lid list hold up the same way? If a parent offered to double the margin in exchange for cutting the fruit, would "listen to the families" survive?

Lesson

Patagonia — the ultimate test (2022)

In September 2022, Chouinard gave the company away:

  • 98% of shares → Holdfast Collective (a nonprofit fighting climate change)
  • 2% → a trust ensuring the mission is enforced
  • All future profit → environmental causes, not owners
Key Point"Earth is now our only shareholder."

Ask: is Patagonia now a business, a social enterprise, or a nonprofit?

Key PointThe decision tree: owners keep profit? → No (surplus → mission) → the structure moved past social enterprise into nonprofit territory — while the stores still sell jackets.
Lesson

Case study — Seesaw Coffee

What happens when a brand abandons its own vision to chase a price war?
Lesson

Seesaw — the vision

Seesaw Coffee was founded in 2012 in Shanghai — one of the pioneers of China's specialty coffee movement.

Its stated vision:

Vision"Bring specialty coffee into everyone's daily life."

Core values: craft, innovation, community — the brand built its identity around quality beans, skilled baristas, and a curated café experience.

Run the testDoes this describe a future worth wanting or the work? It reads like aspiration — a future where specialty coffee is everyday, not premium.
Lesson

Seesaw — the gap

The specialty vision worked — until the 9.9-yuan price war arrived.

  • Luckin Coffee and Cotti flooded the market with deep-discount coupons (9.9 yuan per cup)
  • Seesaw cut prices and ran its own 9.9 promotions to keep customers
  • But specialty beans and trained baristas cost more than 9.9 yuan per cup — every discounted sale was a loss
  • The brand that stood for craft was now competing on price — a war it could not win
Key PointA value the business abandons the first time money appears was a decoration. Seesaw's core value of "craft" was discarded the moment the price war started.
Lesson

Seesaw — the result

YearEvent
2012Founded in Shanghai — specialty coffee pioneer
2017–2021VC-funded expansion; stores in major cities across China
2023–2024Price war with Luckin/Cotti; 9.9-yuan promotions erode margins
2024–2025Mass store closures; debts unpaid; bankruptcy
Key PointThe vision could not survive the first competitive shock because the business stopped acting on it. When the price war arrived, "craft" was the first thing to go — and the brand followed.
Lesson

Seesaw — discussion

Pair up. 2 minutes.

  1. Classify Seesaw's "Bring specialty coffee into everyone's daily life" — vision or mission? Why?
  2. Seesaw's value was craft. At what point did the business stop testing that value at real decisions? What did the 9.9-yuan promotion tell customers?
  3. How is Seesaw's outcome different from Patagonia's? Both faced pressure to compromise — what made the difference?

Three kinds of organizations — at a glance

The difference is what else they do.

🏢

BBusiness

Achieve and increase profits, remain competitive and viable.

Profit: owners keep or reinvest.

🤝

SSocial enterprise

Profit plus a built-in social objective.

Profit: owners keep it.

🌱

NNonprofit

Serve the public good.

Profit: surplus must be reinvested; no owner payment.

Key PointThe decision tree: owners keep profit? → no = nonprofit. Yes + built-in social objective = social enterprise. Yes + no social objective = business.
Lesson

NThe nonprofit signature

Can owners keep the profit at all?
▼
NO → every surplus → mission = NONPROFIT
▼
Surplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.

A nonprofit can sell goods, and revenues can exceed costs — but none of the surplus may flow to an owner.

Lesson

Business — definition

A business seeks to achieve and increase profits, fulfill its stated mission/goals, and remain competitive and viable long-term.

Profit improves in exactly two ways:

  • Increase revenues
  • Decrease costs
Key PointThat's the revenue − cost arithmetic from 1.1, now running a whole organization.
Lesson

Social enterprise — definition

A social enterprise seeks profit while also, by design, achieving social objectives.

Impact travels through three channels:

ChannelExample
The products themselvesA company whose product is a low-cost water filter
OperationsA moving company that hires workers other employers overlook
The financial modelA brand that routes a share of every sale to a local scholarship fund
Lesson

Nonprofit organization — definition

A nonprofit serves the public good rather than generating profit for owners.

Exam NoteMark the precision — the exam tests the edges: a nonprofit can sell goods and services, and its revenues can exceed its costs. The law draws one hard line: any surplus must be reinvested in the organization, and none of it may flow to owners.
Lesson

Nonprofit revenue sources

Nonprofits raise funds through channels that don't require a sale:

- Grant = funding awarded for a stated purpose, usually by a government or foundation; earned by application, not by sale
  • Donations arrive with no product attached
  • Volunteers lower the labor side of the cost ledger
Key PointSurplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.
Lesson

The organization decision tree

Ask two questions in order:

  1. Can owners keep the profit at all?
  • If no (every surplus feeds the mission) → nonprofit
  1. If yes → look for a social objective built into the products, operations, or financial model:
  • Find one → social enterprise
  • Find none → business
Key PointSurplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.

Practice — 6 questions

Read → pause → commit to an answer → then reveal.
3 on one stimulus (Bright Thread)· 3 standalone
Stimulus

Scenario

Rosa Delgado owns Bright Thread, a clothing alteration and repair shop. Posted core values: craftsmanship, honesty, community. Bright Thread is known across the neighborhood for the fastest turnaround of any tailor — a strength built on Rosa's custom scheduling system. Two framed statements hang by the register:

1. "Clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives." 2. "Bright Thread repairs and alters clothing with same-week service at prices working families can afford."

Rosa is weighing two expansions: (1) reselling inexpensive imported garments she has not inspected at a high markup, or (2) launching a same-day uniform repair service for local businesses.

Practice Question
Q1Classify the fastest turnaround

Bright Thread's fastest-in-the-neighborhood turnaround is best classified as which?

AA core value
BA vision statement
CA social objective
DA core competency

Pause. Ask: is "fastest turnaround" something the business believes, or something it is good at?

-->

Answer D

Why

Core competencies are capabilities/skills/expertise that help a business outperform rivals and achieve competitive advantage. Fastest turnaround is a capability with the rival comparison built right into the phrase, resting on a specific skill — Rosa's scheduling system.

A — the paired-concept swap for this topic. Core values** are beliefs/principles (the craftsmanship, honesty, community posted on the wall).
Key PointA value is what the business believes; a competency is what it is good at. Speed here is a built skill → competency.
Practice Question
Q2How values + competencies should guide the choice

Which best explains how Rosa's core values and core competencies should influence her choice between the two expansions?

AChoose the resale option, because higher markups increase profit fastest
BFavor the option that aligns with her values and uses her existing strengths — the same-day uniform service
CPursue both options, because businesses grow by accepting every opportunity
DCore values apply only to employees, so they shouldn't affect an owner's decision
Answer B

Why

Businesses consider core competencies when deciding which opportunities to pursue and how to allocate resources, and choose actions consistent with their values. The uniform service runs directly on her speed + scheduling system and serves the community her values name; reselling uninspected garments strains craftsmanship + honesty while using none of her strengths.

A — high-margin move that abandons strengths/values risks the reputation the profits depend on.**
C — one shop cannot fund and staff every opportunity; choosing well is the entire skill being tested.**
Practice Question
Q3Classify statement 1

The first framed statement — "clothing that lasts, in a neighborhood that thrives" — is best classified as which?

AA vision statement, because it expresses the shop's values and aspirations
BA mission statement, because it describes the shop's daily operations
CA code of conduct for employees
DA business hypothesis to be tested
Answer A

Why

A vision statement is a concise description of core values and aspirations — this names a future the shop wants to exist (durable clothing, thriving neighborhood) and describes no operations.

B — the vision-mission swap. Statement 2 is the mission:** what it does — repairs/alterations — and how — same-week service at fair prices.
Key PointRun the test: a future worth wanting = vision; a description of the work = mission.
Practice Question
Q4Why communicate core values?

Which best explains why businesses communicate core values to their employees?

ATo guarantee that no employee ever behaves unethically
BTo increase revenue in the following quarter
CTo align employees to a shared purpose and guide decisions consistent with the business's beliefs
DTo satisfy a legal requirement that businesses publish their values
Answer C

Why

Businesses communicate core values to align employees to a shared purpose and guide decision makers toward choices consistent with the business's beliefs.

A — "guarantee" is an absolute. Values guide choices; no statement on a wall guarantees behavior.**
D — borrows legal-factor thinking from Topic 1.3** — no law requires a business to publish core values.

Practice Question
Q5What does the data support?

The $30,000 surplus was spent expanding the organization's free cooking classes, and no payment went to any owner. Based on the table, Harbor Kitchen is best classified as which?

AA traditional business, because it earns revenue from selling meals
BA nonprofit organization, because its surplus is reinvested in its mission and it relies partly on grants and donations
CA social enterprise, because it sells meals and offers free classes
DA failing organization, because its costs exceeded its sales revenue
Answer B

Why

Every marker points the same direction: grants and donations alongside sales revenue, the surplus went back into the mission, and no owner received a payment.

Key PointSurplus reinvestment is the legal signature of a nonprofit.
C — the tell is the destination of the money: a social enterprise seeks profit for its owners alongside social objectives; Harbor Kitchen pays no owner anything.**
D — reads one row and stops: sales alone fall short of costs, but total revenue is $280,000 against $250,000 = a $30,000 surplus. A nonprofit running a surplus is healthy, legal, and common.**
Practice Question
Q6Social enterprise vs. traditional business

Which best distinguishes a social enterprise from a traditional business?

AA social enterprise intentionally pursues social objectives through its products, operations, or financial model while still seeking profit
BA social enterprise is legally required to reinvest all surplus funds into the organization
CA social enterprise must be owned by a charity
DA social enterprise always donates the majority of its profits
Answer A

Why

A social enterprise seeks profit while also achieving social objectives; impact travels through three channels: products, operations, or financial model.

B — borrows the nonprofit's legal rule. Reinvestment of all surplus defines nonprofits; social enterprises keep real profit.**
D — fails on "always" and "majority" — a fixed donation share is one possible** financial model, and nothing about the category requires it.
