Lesson

LO 2.2.A — Why consumers buy

EK 2.2.A.1

Consumers buy goods and services to meet basic needs and wants.

The father needs his kid transported to school and wants that on a bike the kid will actually ride.

Most needs have rival goods circling them — multiple products that can meet the same need.

Lesson

Four tiebreakers

When rivals exist, four things break the tie:

💲

Price

A comparable new bike costs about twice Theo's price.

⭐

Differentiated features

The signed inspection tag + 30-day tune-up promise.

⚡

Availability

His bike rolls home today; online order ships next week.

📣

Advertising

The listing that brought him to the stall.

Lesson

Rational vs. habitual decisions

EK 2.2.A.2

How carefully a consumer chooses depends on what a mistake would cost.

  • Consequential purchases (a home, a car, a first bike for a kid riding to school in traffic): consumers use a rational decision-making process — list the alternatives, compare them systematically, pick the best fit
  • Small routine purchases (the coffee on the way to work, the same gum at the same register): run on habit with no comparison at all
Lesson

Worked example — true cost comparison

A parent spends three evenings on listings and finds an untagged $75 rival to Theo's $95 commuter.

The rival comes with no inspection, and a shop charges about $25 to check the brakes.

$$\$75 + \$25 = \$100$$

The tagged bike wins by $5 — even though the sticker price pointed the other way.

Key PointThree evenings of searching surfaced that reversal — a sane trade for a bike and an absurd one for gum.
Lesson

Match depth to stakes

Purchase typeProcessCost of the process
Consequential (bike, car, home)Rational — compare alternatives systematicallyMostly time spent gathering information
Routine (coffee, gum)Habitual — no comparisonAlmost zero
Key PointMatch the depth of the process to the size of the decision.
Lesson

Why rational matters — the search cost

The rational process "charges" for the search mostly in time spent gathering information.

Theo's buyer spent three evenings comparing listings to find the $75 rival. That time investment makes sense for a $95 bike — but would be absurd for a $1.50 pack of gum.

Key PointThe search cost must be proportionate to the stakes. A rational process for every purchase would paralyze the buyer.

Personal factors

EK 2.2.A.3

Facts of the buyer's own life:

Age, sex, education, occupation, income level, budget, lifestyle.

They set what a consumer needs, wants, and can afford.

Key PointRenee's profile card (from 2.1) is a stack of personal factors: 41 years old, a $90 budget, a middle schooler who needs to reach campus by bell time. Her income sets the ceiling; her kid's age sets the frame size.

Social and cultural factors

EK 2.2.A.5

From other people:

Peers, family, social status, cultural norms, media.

They define what feels acceptable, desirable, or appropriate to buy.

Key PointWhen three families at school pickup mention the same stall, buying there becomes the normal move. At the school bike rack, a clean rebuilt commuter carries a little status of its own. The pickup photos Theo posts (with permission) are media doing the same work at scale.
Lesson

Situational influences

EK 2.2.A.6

Temporary conditions around the moment of purchase:

Store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, product availability.

Key PointA flea market is loud and cluttered, so Theo's rack fights the setting: three tiers in a straight line, tags facing out, a clear lane for test rides. A sunny back-to-school Saturday encourages buying; a rainy one discourages it — the bikes are identical both days.
Lesson

Situational — the exam warning

Key PointSituational influences are temporary conditions around the purchase — not features of the product.

The candle shop plays quiet music and keeps the lighting warm → situational (surroundings change for 20 minutes).

The candle is made of soy wax → not situational (that's a product feature).

Key PointIf it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.
Lesson

Exam signal — the four-family question

The exam will describe a scenario and ask: which family of influence?

Method:

  1. Read the scenario — find the factor acting on the buyer
  2. Ask the sorting question — census trait? contents of mind? other people? temporary surroundings?
  3. Eliminate — if it's not a census trait, it's not personal; if other people aren't involved, it's not social
Key Point"Store design" and "lighting" always point to situational. "Values" and "beliefs" always point to psychological. Don't overthink it.

LO 2.2.B — Purchasing patterns

EK 2.2.B.1

A purchasing pattern = a consumer's typical routine for buying, described by:

DimensionWhat it measures
Timingwhen the purchase happens
Frequencyhow often
Quantityhow much per purchase

Where a person lives/works, income level, and the patterns of family/friends all help set it.

Lesson

Purchasing patterns — Theo's ledger

DimensionWhat Theo's ledger shows
Timingclustered in late August (back to school)
Frequencyevery few years per kid (one bike per growth spurt)
Quantityone bike at a time
Key PointLocal families buy one bike at a time, every few years per kid, clustered in late August, from a stall a short drive from home.
Lesson

Purchasing patterns — the exam method

When the exam gives you a data table and asks about purchasing patterns:

  1. Check timing — when do purchases happen? (same time each day? seasonal?)
  2. Check frequency — how often? (daily? weekly? yearly?)
  3. Check quantity — how much per purchase? (one item? bulk?)
  4. If all three are steady → habitual pattern
  5. If any vary → not habitual; possibly rational
Key PointA table that shows consistent timing + frequency + quantity = habitual. Don't add what the table doesn't show.
Lesson

Laws bend patterns

EK 2.2.B.2

Laws regulate what consumers can buy and when/where they can buy it:

  • Many places require helmets for young riders → a kids' bike sale tends to pull a helmet sale along behind it
  • Consumer protection laws help people avoid fraudulent or unsafe products → reselling a recalled children's product can break the law, so checking recall lists is the first point on Theo's 21-point inspection
  • Legal factors also give rise to substitutes — when a product gets harder to obtain, consumers find another way to meet the need
Lesson

Laws create substitutes

When a law makes a product harder to obtain, the need persists and substitutes appear:

LawSubstitutes that appeared
Cities restrict rental scooterssome riders substitute bicycles
Single-use plastic straw banpaper and metal straws fill the gap
Helmet requirement for young ridershelmet sales rise alongside bike sales
Key PointThe law changes availability. The need stays the same. Businesses arrive to serve it with something else.

  • Trued Cycles only exists inside that shift — Theo learned repairs from free videos, sells through a marketplace app, and watches parents check his price against new-bike prices on their phones

LO 2.2.C — Seven principles of influence

EK 2.2.C.1–8

Psychologist Robert Cialdini cataloged the principles of influence — psychological factors that lead people to comply with a request. Marketers build sales tactics on them.

Key PointEvery principle has an honest application and a manipulative one.
Lesson

The seven principles — table

PrincipleWhat it is
Scarcitythe rarer an item seems, the more people want it
Authoritypeople follow authority figures and credentials
Consensus (social proof)people follow the norms of their group
Likingpeople are more easily influenced by someone they like
Reciprocitypeople feel obliged to give back after receiving value
Consistencypeople repeat behavior that matches their self-image
Unitywhen people feel part of a group, the group's influence grows
Lesson

How to sort the seven principles

The exam gives you a scenario and asks which principle. Sort by the mechanism, not the keyword:

PrincipleMechanismSorting question
Scarcityperceived rarityIs the item presented as limited or one-of-a-kind?
Authorityexpert credentialsDoes an expert or credential endorse it?
Consensusmany buyers' behaviorDo many other people already buy or approve?
Likingrapport / relatabilityDoes the seller feel friendly or relatable?
Reciprocityobligation from receivingDid the buyer receive something free first?
Consistencyself-image matchDoes the tactic appeal to who the buyer believes they are?
Unitygroup membershipDoes the buyer feel like part of a group?
Key PointThe word in the answer choice is unreliable. The engine decides.

The Face Test

EK 2.2.C.8

The exam creators will ask you to judge a tactic and build one.

Key PointA tactic is worth keeping when the principle still works after full disclosure.

Theo's test: say the tactic to the customer's face and see if it survives.

Lesson

Face Test — why it works

The face test separates honest persuasion from deception:

Honest tacticManipulative tactic
Disclosuresurvives when the buyer knows how it workscollapses when the buyer knows how it works
Trustbuilds over time — returning shoppers come backerodes — one caught lie undoes months of goodwill
Principleworks because the underlying psychology is realworks only because the buyer is misinformed
Key PointIf the principle needs secrecy to function, it's manipulation. If it works in the open, it's persuasion.
Lesson

Face Test — three examples

TacticFace test
"This tag lists everything I checked"survives — the buyer can read every point
"Come back within 30 days and the tune-up is free"survives — and reverses the risk that keeps used-bike buyers from saying yes
"Three other people are coming this afternoon"dies the moment a buyer asks "who?"
Key PointOne caught lie undoes the trust the signed checklist was built to earn — and trust is what converts these buyers.

Your turn — sample answer

Principle: Reciprocity

Tactic: Theo keeps a pump and chain lube at the stall. Any visitor who rode a bike there gets the tires aired up and the chain lubed — free, no purchase required.

3 on one stimulus (Cobalt Finch Ceramics)· 3 standalone
Stimulus

Scenario

Ivy Santos, a high school senior, runs Cobalt Finch Ceramics, a stall selling handmade mugs at a monthly craft fair. Three other stalls sell mugs, so shoppers can meet the same want four ways and weigh price, glaze, and feel before choosing.

Behind her table Ivy pins a corkboard of short notes from past buyers describing their mugs in daily use (two dozen so far). At the front edge sits a dish of small glazed clay magnets, free to anyone who stops.

Stimulus

Scenario

The vendor at the next stall urges her to tell shoppers each design is the last one available — including designs with duplicates boxed under the table.

Ivy declines the line, and letters a small card reading "one of a kind" for the three mugs whose copper glaze she cannot repeat.

Watch for: consensus (the corkboard), reciprocity (the free magnets), and scarcity (the honest card vs. the faked line).

Practice Question
Q1The corkboard of buyer notes

The corkboard of buyer notes is best identified as a sales tactic built on which principle of influence?

AAuthority, because the notes come from experienced mug owners
BLiking, because the notes make the stall feel friendly
CConsensus, because displayed customer feedback signals that many buyers like the product
DUnity, because reading the notes makes shoppers part of a group

Pause. Ask: what is the mechanism — expertise, rapport, many-buyers, or membership?

-->

Answer C

Why

Consensus = individuals follow the norms of their social group → promoting positive customer feedback creates the sense that most buyers like the product. Two dozen notes from satisfied strangers tell a browsing shopper that people like her already chose these mugs.

A — the test is expertise. Authority persuades through experts and credentials; Ivy's note writers claim nothing beyond owning a mug.
D — overreads membership. Unity works when customers feel part of a group; reading strangers' notes confers no membership.
Key PointConsensus = what many buyers did. Authority = what a credentialed expert says. Unity = makes the buyer a member.
Practice Question
Q2The dish of free magnets

Which best explains how the dish of free magnets can increase mug sales?

AReceiving an item of value makes shoppers feel obligated to give something back, often a purchase
BThe magnets make shoppers like Ivy, and people buy from sellers they like
CThe magnets appeal to shoppers' self-image as supporters of young artists
DThe magnets make the mugs seem rarer than the rival stalls' mugs
Answer A

Why

Reciprocity = individuals feel obligated to reciprocate when given something of value (why marketers offer free samples, trials, gifts). A shopper who pockets a free magnet carries a small felt debt to the stall — buying a mug settles it.

B names a real side effect but the wrong engine: liking persuades through rapport/relatability; a giveaway's tested mechanism is the obligation it plants.
C describes consistency (people repeat behavior matching their self-image); a magnet in a dish makes no identity claim.
Key PointA free item persuades through the obligation it creates = reciprocity.
Practice Question
Q3Refuse the line, keep the cards

Which best explains Ivy's decision to refuse the vendor's line while keeping the one-of-a-kind cards?

AScarcity persuades only when the seller invents the shortage, so the cards cannot motivate buyers
BRefusing the line means Ivy has given up the scarcity principle entirely
CScarcity claims are prohibited at in-person markets
DA true claim of rarity applies the scarcity principle honestly, while the false line risks deception and lost trust
Answer D

Why

Scarcity runs on perceived rarity; real rarity produces that perception honestly — so the card on an unrepeatable glaze keeps the persuasive pull and keeps the claim true. The false version invites the deception consumer protection laws exist to catch, and at a monthly fair with returning shoppers, one duplicate spotted in a box undoes months of corkboard trust.

A — the tell is the absolute. Nothing in the principle requires invention; a genuine shortage is the cleanest source of perceived rarity.
B — fails on the stimulus: Ivy still labels the three unrepeatable mugs, so the principle stays in use where it is true.
Key PointA true rarity claim applies the same principle as a false one — and costs nothing when checked.

Bluestem Coffee (drive-through) pulls one member's loyalty-app records:

Why

A purchasing pattern is a consumer's typical routine, read through timing, frequency, quantity — all three steady here:

Practice Question
Q5The straw ordinance

A city ordinance bars restaurants from handing out single-use plastic straws. Within a year, local stores stock paper and metal straws, and reusable-straw sales climb. Which best explains the change in purchasing patterns?

ALegal restrictions can give rise to substitute products as consumers find new ways to meet an existing need
BThe ordinance eliminated the need the product served, so demand disappeared
CThe change is a situational influence, because store layouts shifted
DConsumer protection laws require every banned product to be replaced by a licensed alternative
Answer A

Why

When a law makes a product harder to obtain, the want it served survives → consumers find substitutes and businesses arrive to sell them. (The straw leaves, the drink stays; paper and metal fill the gap.)

B breaks the chain at the wrong link: the ordinance changed availability, and the underlying want persisted untouched.
C borrows a neighboring factor family: situational influences are temporary (noise, lighting, timing); an ordinance is a legal factor that holds until repealed.
Key PointWhen the law removes a product, the need remains, and substitutes appear to serve it.
Practice Question
Q6The candle shop

A candle shop plays quiet music, keeps the lighting warm, and sets open testers by the entrance so shoppers smell the candles immediately. Shoppers who came in to browse frequently leave with a purchase. Which best identifies the factor influencing these purchases?

AA personal factor (occupation and budget)
BA social and cultural factor (peers set the norm)
CA situational influence (store design creates temporary conditions that encourage buying)
DA psychological factor (the store changes each shopper's values and beliefs)
Answer C

Why

Situational influences = temporary conditions that encourage/discourage buying, created by store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, and product availability. The music, warmth, and testers work on the shopper only while she stands inside them.

D — the test is what changed: the store adjusted the shopper's surroundings for twenty minutes, and her values and beliefs walked out the same as they walked in.**
Key PointIf it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.
