Rational vs. habitual decisions

EK 2.2.A.2

depth of choosing = what a mistake costs

  • consequential (home · car · first bike) → rational process
  • routine (coffee · gum) → habit, no comparison
Lesson

Worked example — true cost comparison

parent spends 3 evenings comparing → finds an untagged $75 rival

rival has no inspection · a brake check costs ~$25

effective rival price = $75 + $25 (check) = $100

tagged bike wins by $5 — sticker said the opposite

Key Point3 evenings of search = sane for a bike, absurd for gum

Purchase typeProcessCost of the process

Routine (coffee, gum)Habitual — no comparisonAlmost zero

rational process charges search time

Situational influences

EK 2.2.A.6

Temporary conditions around the moment of purchase:

Store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, product availability.

Key Pointloud, cluttered market → his rack fights the setting: 3 tiers, tags out
Lesson

Situational — the exam warning

Key Pointsituational = temporary conditions around the purchase, not product features

quiet music + warm lighting → situational (20 minutes)

soy wax → not situational — a product feature

Key PointIf it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.
Lesson

Exam signal — the four-family question

the exam asks: which family?

Method:

  1. Read the scenario — find the factor acting on the buyer
  2. sort: census trait? contents of mind? other people? surroundings?
  3. eliminate: not census → not personal · no people → not social
Key Pointstore design / lighting → situational · values / beliefs → psychological

Purchasing patterns — the exam method

data table + “purchasing pattern”?

  1. timing — same hour? seasonal?
  2. Check frequency — how often? (daily? weekly? yearly?)
  3. Check quantity — how much per purchase? (one item? bulk?)
  4. If all three are steady → habitual pattern
  5. If any vary → not habitual; possibly rational
Key Pointconsistent timing + frequency + quantity = habitual · don't add what the table doesn't show

Practice — 6 questions

Read → pause → commit to an answer → then reveal.
3 on one stimulus (Cobalt Finch Ceramics)· 3 standalone
Stimulus

Scenario

Ivy Santos, a high school senior, runs Cobalt Finch Ceramics, a stall selling handmade mugs at a monthly craft fair. Three other stalls sell mugs, so shoppers can meet the same want four ways and weigh price, glaze, and feel before choosing.

Behind her table Ivy pins a corkboard of short notes from past buyers describing their mugs in daily use (two dozen so far). At the front edge sits a dish of small glazed clay magnets, free to anyone who stops.

Stimulus

Scenario

The vendor at the next stall urges her to tell shoppers each design is the last one available — including designs with duplicates boxed under the table.

Ivy declines the line, and letters a small card reading "one of a kind" for the three mugs whose copper glaze she cannot repeat.

Watch for: consensus (the corkboard), reciprocity (the free magnets), and scarcity (the honest card vs. the faked line).

Practice Question
Q1The corkboard of buyer notes

The corkboard of buyer notes is best identified as a sales tactic built on which principle of influence?

AAuthority, because the notes come from experienced mug owners
BLiking, because the notes make the stall feel friendly
CConsensus, because displayed customer feedback signals that many buyers like the product
DUnity, because reading the notes makes shoppers part of a group

Pause — the mechanism: expertise? rapport? many buyers? membership?

Answer C

Why

Consensus = individuals follow the norms of their social group → promoting positive customer feedback creates the sense that most buyers like the product. Two dozen notes from satisfied strangers tell a browsing shopper that people like her already chose these mugs.

A — the test is expertise. Authority persuades through experts and credentials; Ivy's note writers claim nothing beyond owning a mug.
D — overreads membership. Unity works when customers feel part of a group; reading strangers' notes confers no membership.
Key PointConsensus = what many buyers did. Authority = what a credentialed expert says. Unity = makes the buyer a member.
Practice Question
Q2The dish of free magnets

Which best explains how the dish of free magnets can increase mug sales?

AReceiving an item of value makes shoppers feel obligated to give something back, often a purchase
BThe magnets make shoppers like Ivy, and people buy from sellers they like
CThe magnets appeal to shoppers' self-image as supporters of young artists
DThe magnets make the mugs seem rarer than the rival stalls' mugs
Answer A

Why

Reciprocity = individuals feel obligated to reciprocate when given something of value (why marketers offer free samples, trials, gifts). A shopper who pockets a free magnet carries a small felt debt to the stall — buying a mug settles it.

B names a real side effect but the wrong engine: liking persuades through rapport/relatability; a giveaway's tested mechanism is the obligation it plants.
C describes consistency (people repeat behavior matching their self-image); a magnet in a dish makes no identity claim.
Key PointA free item persuades through the obligation it creates = reciprocity.
Practice Question
Q3Refuse the line, keep the cards

Which best explains Ivy's decision to refuse the vendor's line while keeping the one-of-a-kind cards?

AScarcity persuades only when the seller invents the shortage, so the cards cannot motivate buyers
BRefusing the line means Ivy has given up the scarcity principle entirely
CScarcity claims are prohibited at in-person markets
DA true claim of rarity applies the scarcity principle honestly, while the false line risks deception and lost trust
Answer D

Why

Scarcity runs on perceived rarity; real rarity produces that perception honestly — so the card on an unrepeatable glaze keeps the persuasive pull and keeps the claim true. The false version invites the deception consumer protection laws exist to catch, and at a monthly fair with returning shoppers, one duplicate spotted in a box undoes months of corkboard trust.

A — the tell is the absolute. Nothing in the principle requires invention; a genuine shortage is the cleanest source of perceived rarity.
B — fails on the stimulus: Ivy still labels the three unrepeatable mugs, so the principle stays in use where it is true.
Key PointA true rarity claim applies the same principle as a false one — and costs nothing when checked.

Q4Purchasing-pattern table

Bluestem Coffee (drive-through) pulls one member's loyalty-app records:

Week 1Weeks 2–4

Which conclusion is supported by the table?

CThe member's lifetime value to Bluestem is $80

Why

A purchasing pattern is a consumer's typical routine, read through timing, frequency, quantity — all three steady here:

Practice Question
Q5The straw ordinance

A city ordinance bars restaurants from handing out single-use plastic straws. Within a year, local stores stock paper and metal straws, and reusable-straw sales climb. Which best explains the change in purchasing patterns?

ALegal restrictions can give rise to substitute products as consumers find new ways to meet an existing need
BThe ordinance eliminated the need the product served, so demand disappeared
CThe change is a situational influence, because store layouts shifted
DConsumer protection laws require every banned product to be replaced by a licensed alternative
Answer A

Why

When a law makes a product harder to obtain, the want it served survives → consumers find substitutes and businesses arrive to sell them. (The straw leaves, the drink stays; paper and metal fill the gap.)

B breaks the chain at the wrong link: the ordinance changed availability, and the underlying want persisted untouched.
C borrows a neighboring factor family: situational influences are temporary (noise, lighting, timing); an ordinance is a legal factor that holds until repealed.
Key PointWhen the law removes a product, the need remains, and substitutes appear to serve it.
Practice Question
Q6The candle shop

A candle shop plays quiet music, keeps the lighting warm, and sets open testers by the entrance so shoppers smell the candles immediately. Shoppers who came in to browse frequently leave with a purchase. Which best identifies the factor influencing these purchases?

AA personal factor (occupation and budget)
BA social and cultural factor (peers set the norm)
CA situational influence (store design creates temporary conditions that encourage buying)
DA psychological factor (the store changes each shopper's values and beliefs)
Answer C

Why

Situational influences = temporary conditions that encourage/discourage buying, created by store design (noise, lighting, organization), timing, and product availability. The music, warmth, and testers work on the shopper only while she stands inside them.

D — the test is what changed: the store adjusted her surroundings for twenty minutes, and her values / beliefs walked out unchanged.
Key PointIf it changes when the shopper walks out the door, it's situational. If it travels home with the product, it isn't.
